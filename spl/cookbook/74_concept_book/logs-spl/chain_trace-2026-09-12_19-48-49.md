### proton (35.1s)

## Proton

A proton is a subatomic particle carrying one elementary unit of positive electric charge, $q_p = +e \approx 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, and a rest mass of $m_p \approx 1.673 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, roughly 1,836 times the mass of an electron. Protons, together with neutrons, constitute the atomic nucleus; the number of protons in a nucleus — the atomic number $Z$ — uniquely determines the chemical identity of an element and fixes the nucleus's total positive charge, $Q = Ze$.

**Worked example.** Consider a helium-4 nucleus ($^4_2\text{He}$), containing $Z = 2$ protons. By the relation above, the nucleus carries total charge
$$
Q = Ze = 2 \times (1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}) \approx 3.204 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}.
$$
This charge is what a nucleus presents to orbiting electrons and to other nuclei during collisions; it is set entirely by the proton count, independent of how many neutrons accompany those protons.

**Key theorem (conservation of charge in nuclear reactions).** In any isolated nuclear reaction, total electric charge is conserved:
$$
\sum Z_{\text{before}}\, e = \sum Z_{\text{after}}\, e,
$$
so the number of protons contributed by the reactants must equal the number of protons appearing among the products, once any charged decay particles are included in the count. This principle constrains which nuclear transformations — such as proton–proton fusion in stellar cores — are permissible.

**Lab cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational

e = Rational('1.602176634') * Rational(10) ** -19  # elementary charge, C
Z = 2  # protons in a helium-4 nucleus

Q = Z * e
print(f"Nuclear charge for Z={Z}: {float(Q):.4e} C")
```

### atom (28.9s)

## Atom

An **atom** is the smallest unit of matter that retains the chemical identity of an element. Formally, an atom consists of a dense, positively charged nucleus—composed of $Z$ protons and $N$ neutrons—surrounded by a cloud of $Z$ electrons (in a neutral atom) occupying quantized energy states. The number of protons, $Z$, defines the element; the total number of protons plus neutrons, $A = Z + N$, gives the mass number. Two atoms of the same element may differ in $N$ while sharing the same $Z$.

**Worked example.** Consider carbon-12, $^{12}_{6}\text{C}$. Here $Z = 6$ (six protons, hence six electrons in the neutral atom) and $A = 12$, so $N = A - Z = 6$ neutrons. The nuclear charge is $+6e$, where $e \approx 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ is the elementary charge. Since electrons carry charge $-e$ each, six electrons balance the nuclear charge exactly, yielding a net atomic charge of zero—consistent with the defining property of a neutral atom.

**Key theorem (charge neutrality).** For any atom with $Z$ protons and $Z$ electrons, the total charge $Q$ satisfies
$$
Q = Z(+e) + Z(-e) = 0.
$$
This result follows directly from the definition of a neutral atom and confirms that atomic structure, though composed of charged constituents, produces no net electric charge—an essential fact for understanding how atoms combine into molecules without spontaneous electrostatic repulsion.

**Lab cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational

Z, N = 6, 6
A = Z + N
e = 1.602e-19  # elementary charge, C

nuclear_charge = Z * e
electron_charge = -Z * e
net_charge = nuclear_charge + electron_charge

print(f"Mass number A = {A}")
print(f"Net atomic charge = {net_charge:.3e} C")  # expect ~0
```

### neutron (27.9s)

## Neutron

**Definition.** A neutron is a subatomic particle with electric charge $q = 0$ and rest mass $m_n \approx 1.6749 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} \approx 1.00867\ \text{u}$, found together with protons in atomic nuclei. Together with the number of protons $Z$ (atomic number), the number of neutrons $N$ in a nucleus determines the mass number $A = Z + N$. Nuclei with the same $Z$ but different $N$ are isotopes of the same element; the neutron is thus the quantity that distinguishes isotopes from one another, since $Z$ alone fixes the element.

**Worked example.** Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both isotopes of carbon ($Z = 6$), differing only in neutron number. For $^{12}\text{C}$: $A = 12$, so $N = A - Z = 12 - 6 = 6$ neutrons. For $^{14}\text{C}$: $N = 14 - 6 = 8$ neutrons. The extra two neutrons make $^{14}\text{C}$ unstable; it decays by converting a neutron into a proton plus an emitted electron,
$$
n \rightarrow p^+ + e^-,
$$
with a half-life of about $5730$ years — the basis of radiocarbon dating.

**Key theorem (mass–energy balance).** In any such nuclear process, total mass–energy is conserved: for reactants and products with rest masses $m_i$,
$$
\sum_i m_i c^2 = \sum_f m_f c^2 + Q,
$$
where $Q$ is the released kinetic energy. Because a free neutron ($m_n c^2 = 939.565\ \text{MeV}$) has slightly more mass–energy than a proton plus electron ($m_p c^2 + m_e c^2 \approx 938.783\ \text{MeV}$), the decay of a free neutron releases $Q \approx 0.782\ \text{MeV}$. This confirms that a free neutron is unstable, in contrast to neutrons bound within most stable nuclei.

**Lab cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import symbols, Rational

m_n, m_p, m_e, c = symbols('m_n m_p m_e c', positive=True)
Q = (m_n - m_p - m_e) * c**2

values = {m_n: 939.565, m_p: 938.272, m_e: 0.511, c: 1}  # MeV/c^2 units
print(Q.subs(values))  # released energy in MeV
```

### atomic_number (22.6s)

## Atomic Number

**Definition.** The atomic number of a chemical element, denoted $Z$, is the number of protons contained in the nucleus of every atom of that element. Because atoms in their neutral state carry no net electric charge, $Z$ also equals the number of electrons occupying the surrounding orbitals. The atomic number is the defining property of an element: two atoms with the same $Z$ belong to the same element, while atoms with different $Z$ values are necessarily different elements. No atom of the same element can have a different proton count than another atom of that element.

**Worked example.** Consider an atom with $6$ protons in its nucleus. By definition its atomic number is $Z = 6$, identifying it unambiguously as carbon. In a neutral carbon atom, there are also exactly $Z = 6$ electrons, arranged as $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^2$. Any atom found in nature with $6$ protons — regardless of any other property of its nucleus — is classified as carbon, since it is the proton count alone that fixes elemental identity.

**Key theorem.** *(Uniqueness of elemental identity).* Let $X$ and $Y$ be atoms with atomic numbers $Z_X$ and $Z_Y$. Then $X$ and $Y$ are atoms of the same chemical element if and only if $Z_X = Z_Y$. The forward direction follows from the periodic law: chemical behavior is governed by electron configuration, which is determined entirely by $Z$ for a neutral atom. The converse holds because elements are classified by proton count by definition (per IUPAC convention).

**Lab cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import symbols, Eq

Z = symbols('Z', positive=True, integer=True)

# Elemental identity is a function of Z alone: two atoms match
# in element exactly when their Z values are equal.
def same_element(Z_X, Z_Y):
    return Eq(Z_X, Z_Y)

carbon_atom_1 = 6  # protons observed in atom 1
carbon_atom_2 = 6  # protons observed in atom 2

print(same_element(carbon_atom_1, carbon_atom_2))  # True
```

### mass_number (26.3s)

## Mass Number

**Definition.** The mass number of an atom, denoted $A$, is the total count of nucleons—protons and neutrons—contained in its nucleus:
$$
A = Z + N,
$$
where $Z$ is the atomic number (number of protons) and $N$ is the number of neutrons. Unlike atomic mass, which is measured in atomic mass units (amu) and reflects the actual physical mass of an atom, the mass number is a dimensionless integer used to identify a specific isotope. An isotope is conventionally denoted $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, or more casually as X-$A$ (e.g., carbon-14).

**Worked example.** Consider an atom of chlorine with $Z = 17$ protons and $N = 18$ neutrons. Its mass number is
$$
A = 17 + 18 = 35,
$$
identifying it as chlorine-35, $^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}$. A second chlorine isotope with $N = 20$ has $A = 37$, giving $^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$. Both isotopes share the same $Z$ (defining the element) but differ in $N$ and hence in $A$.

**Key theorem (isotope relation).** For a fixed element, every naturally occurring isotope shares the same $Z$ but may have a different $N$, and therefore a different mass number $A$. Because $A$ is computed independently for each isotope, the set of mass numbers observed for an element—such as $\{35, 37\}$ for chlorine—need not be consecutive integers, and no single mass number is "the" mass of the element; each merely labels one isotopic form.

**Lab cell (SymPy).**
```python
from sympy import symbols, Eq, solve

Z, N, A = symbols('Z N A', positive=True, integer=True)
mass_number_eq = Eq(A, Z + N)

# Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37: same Z, different N
for N_val in (18, 20):
    A_val = solve(mass_number_eq.subs({Z: 17, N: N_val}), A)[0]
    print(f"Z=17, N={N_val}  ->  A={A_val}")
# Output:
# Z=17, N=18  ->  A=35
# Z=17, N=20  ->  A=37
```

### isotope (18.0s)

## Isotope

An **isotope** is one of two or more forms of a chemical element whose atoms share the same number of protons (atomic number, $Z$) but differ in the number of neutrons, and consequently in mass number, $A = Z + N$. Because chemical behavior is governed almost entirely by the number and configuration of electrons — which in a neutral atom equals $Z$ — isotopes of an element occupy the same position on the periodic table and display nearly identical chemical properties, while their physical properties (mass, and often nuclear stability) differ.

**Worked example.** Carbon has $Z = 6$. Its three naturally occurring isotopes are $^{12}\text{C}$ ($N=6$), $^{13}\text{C}$ ($N=7$), and $^{14}\text{C}$ ($N=8$). The atomic mass of an element listed on the periodic table, such as carbon's $12.011\,\text{u}$, is a weighted average over these isotopes according to their natural abundances $f_i$:
$$
\bar{A} = \sum_i f_i A_i = (0.9893)(12.000) + (0.0107)(13.003) \approx 12.011.
$$
Radioactive $^{14}\text{C}$, though present only in trace amounts, decays over time and underlies radiocarbon dating of organic material.

**Key theorem (mass–abundance relation).** For an element with isotopes of mass $A_i$ and fractional abundance $f_i$ satisfying $\sum_i f_i = 1$, the standard atomic weight equals the abundance-weighted mean $\bar{A} = \sum_i f_i A_i$. This follows directly from the definition of a weighted average applied to a large sample of atoms, and it explains why atomic weights on the periodic table are rarely integers.

**Lab cell (SymPy).**
```python
import sympy as sp

f, A = sp.symbols('f A')
abundances = {12: sp.Rational(9893, 10000), 13: sp.Rational(107, 10000)}
masses = {12: sp.Float(12.000), 13: sp.Float(13.003)}

atomic_weight = sum(abundances[k] * masses[k] for k in abundances)
print(sp.N(atomic_weight, 6))  # 12.0110
```

### radioisotope (26.1s)

## Radioisotope

**Definition.** A radioisotope (or radionuclide) is a variant of a chemical element whose nucleus is unstable and spontaneously decays, emitting radiation and transforming into a more stable daughter isotope. Each radioisotope decays at a characteristic exponential rate independent of temperature, pressure, or chemical bonding, described by

$$
N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t},
$$

where $N_0$ is the initial number of radioactive nuclei, $\lambda$ is the decay constant, and $t$ is elapsed time. The decay constant sets the half-life, $t_{1/2} = \ln 2 / \lambda$, the time required for half of a sample to decay.

**Worked example.** Carbon-14 has a half-life $t_{1/2} = 5{,}730$ years. Its decay constant is $\lambda = \ln 2 / t_{1/2} \approx 1.21 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$. If a fossil sample retains 25% of its original $^{14}\text{C}$, then $N(t)/N_0 = 0.25$, so
$$
t = -\frac{\ln(0.25)}{\lambda} = \frac{\ln 4}{1.21\times10^{-4}} \approx 11{,}460\ \text{years},
$$
which is exactly two half-lives, consistent with a fourfold reduction.

**Key theorem.** The exponential decay law follows from treating decay as a memoryless, first-order process: the probability that any given nucleus decays in an infinitesimal interval $dt$ is $\lambda\,dt$, independent of the nucleus's history. Solving $dN/dt = -\lambda N$ yields $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$. This memoryless property is the theoretical foundation of radiometric dating techniques such as radiocarbon dating.

**Lab cell (SymPy).**
```python
import sympy as sp

t, lam, N0, half_life = sp.symbols('t lambda N0 t_half', positive=True)

lam_expr = sp.ln(2) / half_life
N_t = N0 * sp.exp(-lam_expr * t)

decay_law = sp.Eq(sp.Function('N')(t).diff(t), -lam * sp.Function('N')(t))
solution = sp.dsolve(decay_law, sp.Function('N')(t))

age = sp.solve(sp.Eq(N_t.subs(half_life, 5730), N0 * sp.Rational(1, 4)), t)
print(solution, age)
```

### half_life (9.8s)

## Half Life

**Definition.** Let $N(t)$ denote the quantity of a radioactive substance remaining at time $t$, governed by exponential decay:
$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N, \qquad N(0) = N_0,
$$
where $\lambda > 0$ is the decay constant characteristic of the isotope. The **half-life** $t_{1/2}$ is the time required for the quantity to reduce to half its initial value: $N(t_{1/2}) = N_0/2$.

**Proof sketch.** Separating variables and integrating the decay equation yields $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$. Setting $N(t_{1/2}) = N_0/2$ gives
$$
e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2} \implies -\lambda t_{1/2} = \ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\ln 2,
$$
so
$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}.
$$
This relation shows that $t_{1/2}$ is independent of $N_0$: every sample of the same isotope, regardless of size, loses half its mass in the same fixed interval.

**Worked example.** Carbon-14 has a decay constant $\lambda \approx 1.21 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$. Its half-life is
$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{1.21 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 5{,}730 \text{ years}.
$$
If a fossil sample contains $25\%$ of its original carbon-14, we solve $0.25 = e^{-\lambda t}$, giving $t = \dfrac{\ln 4}{\lambda} \approx 11{,}460$ years — exactly two half-lives, consistent with the identity $N(t) = N_0 (1/2)^{t/t_{1/2}}$.

**Key theorem.** For any exponential decay process, the half-life and decay constant satisfy $t_{1/2}\lambda = \ln 2$, a fixed relationship independent of the initial quantity, elapsed time, or measurement scale.

**Lab cell (SymPy).**
```python
import sympy as sp

t, lam, N0, t_half = sp.symbols('t lambda N0 t_half', positive=True)
N = N0 * sp.exp(-lam * t)

half_life_expr = sp.solve(sp.Eq(N.subs(t, t_half), N0/2), t_half)[0]
print("t_1/2 =", sp.simplify(half_life_expr))  # ln(2)/lambda

lam_val = 1.21e-4
print("Carbon-14 half-life (yr):", half_life_expr.subs(lam, lam_val).evalf())
```

### carbon_dating (9.6s)

## Carbon Dating

Carbon dating is a radiometric method for estimating the age of organic material by measuring the residual proportion of carbon-14 ($^{14}\mathrm{C}$), a radioactive isotope of carbon that decays via beta emission with a well-characterized half-life. While an organism is alive, it continuously exchanges carbon with its environment, maintaining a roughly constant ratio of $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ to stable $^{12}\mathrm{C}$. Once the organism dies, this exchange ceases, and the $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ present begins to decay according to the exponential law
$$N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t},$$
where $N_0$ is the initial quantity of $^{14}\mathrm{C}$, $\lambda$ is the decay constant, and $t$ is elapsed time since death.

**Worked example.** The half-life of $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ is $t_{1/2} = 5730$ years, giving $\lambda = \ln 2 / t_{1/2} \approx 1.21 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$. Suppose a fossil sample retains 35% of its original $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ activity. Solving $0.35 = e^{-\lambda t}$ yields
$$t = \frac{-\ln(0.35)}{\lambda} \approx \frac{1.0498}{1.21 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 8676 \text{ years}.$$

**Key theorem.** *Uniqueness of the decay-based age estimate.* Since $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ is strictly monotonic decreasing for $\lambda > 0$, the map from the fraction remaining $N(t)/N_0 \in (0,1]$ to elapsed time $t$ is a bijection. Consequently, given an accurate measurement of the remaining fraction and known $\lambda$, the elapsed time $t$ is uniquely determined — the mathematical foundation guaranteeing that carbon dating yields a single, well-defined age rather than multiple candidate solutions.

**Lab cell (SymPy).**
```python
import sympy as sp

t, lam, N0, N = sp.symbols('t lambda N0 N', positive=True)
half_life = 5730
lam_val = sp.log(2) / half_life

fraction_remaining = sp.Rational(35, 100)
age = sp.solve(sp.Eq(fraction_remaining, sp.exp(-lam * t)), t)[0]
age_years = age.subs(lam, lam_val)
print(sp.N(age_years, 6))  # ~8676.29 years
```
