## Karyotype

A karyotype is an organized visual profile of an individual's complete set of chromosomes, arranged by size and stained banding pattern. To produce one, cells (typically white blood cells or, prenatally, cells from amniotic fluid) are arrested at the point in cell division when chromosomes are most tightly coiled and visible under a microscope. They are stained, photographed, and digitally sorted into matching pairs, largest to smallest, with the two sex chromosomes placed last. The result is a standardized chart that reveals both how many chromosomes a person has and the visible structure of each one.

A typical human karyotype shows 46 chromosomes: 22 matching pairs plus two sex chromosomes (XX or XY). Each chromosome is identified by its size, by where its pinched "waist" sits along its length, and by a unique pattern of light and dark stripes produced by the stain. These stripes act like a fingerprint, letting geneticists tell one chromosome from another and spot when a piece of one has attached to a different chromosome entirely.

Karyotyping is a diagnostic tool, not a sequencing method—it reveals large, chromosome-level changes rather than changes within individual genes. Consider a patient whose karyotype shows 47 chromosomes, with three copies of chromosome 21 instead of two: this extra copy is the cause of Down syndrome, and it is visible directly on the chart, no DNA sequencing required. Similarly, a karyotype might show that a chunk of one chromosome has swapped places with a chunk of another—a swap associated with certain leukemias—or that a chromosome is visibly shorter than its matching partner because a segment is missing.

When approaching a karyotype problem, work through three questions in order: (1) Count total chromosomes—is it 46, or are there too many or too few? (2) Compare each matching pair—are they equal in length and stripe pattern, or does one show a missing, duplicated, or swapped segment? (3) Examine the sex chromosomes—XX, XY, or an unusual combination such as one extra X or one missing X, each associated with a distinct genetic condition? This systematic scan lets you localize a chromosomal abnormality without needing molecular-level data.

## Karyogram

A karyogram is a standardized photographic or digital chart that displays an individual's full set of chromosomes, arranged in homologous pairs and ordered by size and centromere position. To produce one, cells (typically from blood, amniotic fluid, or bone marrow) are arrested during metaphase, when chromosomes are maximally condensed and visible under a microscope. They are stained—most commonly with Giemsa dye, producing "G-banding"—photographed, and then digitally cut and rearranged into 22 pairs of autosomes (numbered 1 through 22, largest to smallest) plus the sex chromosomes (XX or XY). The resulting layout is the karyogram, while "karyotype" refers to the chromosomal makeup itself.

The banding pattern is the diagnostic key. Each chromosome has a reproducible sequence of light and dark bands, and every band has a name (e.g., 15q11.2) that geneticists use to pinpoint locations precisely. A clinician reading a karyogram checks three things: chromosome number, chromosome structure, and band pattern continuity.

Consider a worked example: a karyogram from a child with developmental delay shows 47 chromosomes instead of 46, with three copies of chromosome 21 instead of two. This is trisomy 21, the chromosomal basis of Down syndrome. In another case, a karyogram shows 46 chromosomes, but chromosome 5 is visibly shorter than its pair, missing a segment of its short arm (5p). This deletion causes cri-du-chat syndrome. In both cases, the diagnosis rests on comparing the patient's chromosomes against a reference karyotype—no genetic sequencing is required, only careful visual and positional analysis.

For problem-solving practice, imagine you receive a karyogram showing 45 chromosomes, with what appears to be a single fused chromosome carrying the banding patterns of both chromosome 14 and chromosome 21. How would you classify this abnormality, and would you expect it to cause clinical symptoms in the carrier? This is a Robertsonian translocation: two chromosomes have fused near their centromeres. Because no genetic material is actually lost—it is rearranged rather than deleted—a carrier is often phenotypically normal. The clinical risk instead falls on their offspring, who may inherit an unbalanced chromosome complement, illustrating why karyogram interpretation must distinguish balanced structural changes from those involving true loss or gain of material.
