Synapsid Anapsid Diapsid

Amniotes—the vertebrates whose eggs are protected by an internal membrane system, freeing them from an aquatic larval stage—diversified early into three lineages distinguished by the pattern of openings, or fenestrae, in the temporal region of the skull behind the eye socket. These openings are not decorative; they mark attachment points and clearance spaces for jaw musculature, so their number and position directly reflect how an animal bites and chews. A synapsid skull has a single temporal fenestra low on each side of the skull. An anapsid skull has none at all, presenting a solid bony wall behind the orbit. A diapsid skull has two fenestrae on each side, one upper and one lower, separated by a bony arch.

These categories are not just descriptive labels; they are the founding branches of the amniote family tree. Synapsids gave rise to mammals, and the single opening in their skulls is why your own jaw muscles bulge and flex the way they do—modern mammals inherited and elaborated this synapsid architecture, including the further division of the jaw joint that let the jawbone's ancestral elements migrate into the middle ear as tiny hearing ossicles. Diapsids gave rise to the reptile lineage broadly, including lizards, snakes, crocodilians, and—critically—birds, whose skulls retain modified diapsid fenestration. Anapsids were long thought to include turtles, since turtle skulls appear solid-walled, but genetic and detailed fossil evidence now show turtles are nested within the diapsid lineage, having secondarily lost their fenestrae; true anapsids are essentially confined to early Paleozoic reptiles that left no living descendants.

Applying this to fossil identification is a genuine problem-solving skill: given an unlabeled amniote skull, counting temporal openings and locating them relative to the postorbital and squamosal bones lets you place the specimen on the amniote tree before ever comparing teeth or limbs. This is also a caution against relying on a single trait—turtles look anapsid but are phylogenetically diapsid, so classification claims must be checked against molecular and multiple morphological lines of evidence rather than one visible skull feature alone.
