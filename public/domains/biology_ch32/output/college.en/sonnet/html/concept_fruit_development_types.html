Fruit Development Types

A fruit is the mature, ripened ovary of a flower, often together with tissues that surrounded it. After fertilization, hormonal signals (especially auxin from developing seeds) trigger the ovary wall — the pericarp — to enlarge, while the ovule matures into a seed. Botanists classify fruits by how many flowers and ovaries contributed to the structure: simple fruits come from one ovary in one flower; aggregate fruits come from many ovaries in one flower; multiple fruits come from many ovaries in many flowers fused into one structure; and accessory fruits derive most of their flesh from tissue outside the ovary altogether.

Worked example. Compare a peach, a raspberry, a pineapple, and an apple. A peach forms from a single ovary with one carpel, so it is a simple fruit (specifically a drupe, with a fleshy exocarp/mesocarp and a hardened, stony endocarp around the seed). A raspberry flower has many separate ovaries (pistils) clustered on one receptacle; each ovary ripens into a tiny drupelet, and the drupelets stay attached to each other — an aggregate fruit. A pineapple develops from an entire inflorescence: dozens of individual flowers, each with its own ovary, fuse together around a central stem as they ripen into one unit — a multiple fruit. An apple's fleshy part is mostly the swollen floral tube (hypanthium) surrounding the ovary, not the ovary wall itself, which forms only the thin papery core around the seeds — an accessory fruit.

Problem-solving application. Given an unfamiliar fruit, you can classify it by tracing its floral origin rather than memorizing categories. Ask three questions in sequence: (1) How many flowers produced this structure — one or many? If many, check whether the ovaries are fused into a single mass (multiple fruit, like a fig or mulberry) or remain distinct. (2) If one flower, how many ovaries (carpels) did it have — one or several? Several unfused carpels ripening together on one receptacle signal an aggregate fruit (as in strawberry or blackberry). (3) Does the edible flesh come mainly from the ovary wall, or from surrounding floral tissue like the receptacle or floral tube? If the latter dominates, it's accessory. This diagnostic sequence — flower count, ovary count, tissue origin — lets you correctly classify fruits like tomatoes, figs, or cashews even without prior knowledge of the specific plant family.
