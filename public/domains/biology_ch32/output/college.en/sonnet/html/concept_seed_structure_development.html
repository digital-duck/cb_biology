Seed Structure Development

After fertilization, the ovule transforms into a seed, and each of its internal tissues takes on a specific role in protecting and nourishing the next generation of the plant. The outer integuments of the ovule harden into the seed coat, a protective shell that shields the embryo from mechanical damage, pathogens, and premature water loss. Inside, the fertilized egg develops into the embryo, a miniature plant already possessing a rudimentary root (radicle), shoot (plumule), and one or two seed leaves called cotyledons. Alongside the embryo, the endosperm forms from the triple fusion of a sperm cell with two polar nuclei in the central cell, producing a triploid ($3n$) tissue that stores starch, protein, and oil.

The key distinction in seed anatomy lies in how monocots and dicots manage this stored food. In dicots, such as beans, the endosperm is largely absorbed during embryo development, and its nutrients are transferred into two thick, fleshy cotyledons, which then serve as the primary food reserve for germination. In monocots, such as corn, the endosperm persists as a separate, prominent tissue, while the single thin cotyledon (called the scutellum) acts as an absorptive organ that channels nutrients from the endosperm to the growing embryo rather than storing them itself.

Consider a practical diagnostic problem: given an unlabeled dry seed cut in cross-section, how would you determine whether it came from a monocot or a dicot? First, look for a large, distinct starchy tissue mass surrounding a small embryo — its presence indicates persistent endosperm and points to a monocot. Second, check the number of cotyledons visible in the embryo: one thin, sheath-like structure indicates a monocot, while two thick, symmetrical fleshy lobes indicate a dicot. Third, iodine solution can confirm this diagnosis chemically: since iodine turns starch blue-black, staining the seed will darken the endosperm in a corn kernel but will darken the cotyledons themselves in a bean seed, since that is where the dicot's starch reserves have relocated. This structural difference is not merely academic — it explains agricultural handling differences, since monocot grains like rice and wheat store food separately from the embryo and can be milled to remove the endosperm (as in white rice) without immediately killing the embryo's viability for a short period, whereas removing a dicot's cotyledons destroys its food supply directly.
