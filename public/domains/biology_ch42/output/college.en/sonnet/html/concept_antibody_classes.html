Antibody Classes

An antibody, or immunoglobulin (Ig), is a Y-shaped protein produced by B cells that binds a specific antigen. All antibodies share the same basic four-chain structure — two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains — but the heavy chain's constant region comes in five distinct versions. This variation defines the five antibody classes: IgM, IgG, IgA, IgD, and IgE. Because the constant region determines how an antibody behaves once it binds its target — where it travels in the body, what immune cells it recruits, whether it fixes complement — class is the single feature that predicts an antibody's job.

Worked example. Consider a person exposed to a new virus. The first antibodies detectable in their blood are IgM: large, five-unit (pentameric) molecules that appear within days, activate complement powerfully, and serve as the initial responder before the immune system has "learned" the antigen well. Within one to two weeks, B cells undergo class switching, and IgG — the smallest, most abundant class — becomes dominant. IgG diffuses efficiently into tissues, neutralizes toxins and pathogens, and is the only class that crosses the placenta, giving a newborn passive immunity. Meanwhile, IgA, secreted as a dimer, guards mucosal surfaces (gut, respiratory tract, breast milk) where it intercepts pathogens before they invade tissue. IgE, though present in trace amounts, binds mast cells and triggers histamine release — essential against parasitic worms, but also the culprit in allergic reactions. IgD's role is more limited: it mainly sits on the surface of naive B cells, helping regulate their activation.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a patient's blood test shows high IgM but low IgG against a specific pathogen. What can you infer? Because IgM appears early and IgG rises later during a sustained or repeat response, this pattern indicates a recent, first-time (primary) infection rather than a past or chronic one. Clinicians use exactly this logic — comparing IgM and IgG titers — to distinguish acute infections from prior immunity, decide whether a vaccine "took," or determine if a fetus was infected in utero (since maternal IgG crosses the placenta but IgM does not, any IgM found in a newborn's blood must be the infant's own).
