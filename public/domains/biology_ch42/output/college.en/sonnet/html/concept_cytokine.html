Cytokine

A cytokine is a small signaling protein secreted by cells—most notably immune cells like macrophages and T lymphocytes, but also fibroblasts and endothelial cells—that binds specific receptors on target cells to regulate their differentiation, proliferation, gene expression, and movement. Cytokines act as the immune system's communication network: they don't attack pathogens directly, but they coordinate which cells respond, how aggressively, and where.

Cytokines are grouped by function. Interleukins (e.g., IL-2, IL-6) mediate communication between white blood cells. Interferons (e.g., IFN-γ) trigger antiviral states in cells and activate immune defenses. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α) drives inflammation and can induce cell death in infected or cancerous cells. Chemokines are a specialized subclass that create concentration gradients guiding immune cells toward infection sites—a process called chemotaxis.

Worked example. Consider a skin wound that becomes infected. Macrophages at the site detect bacterial components and release IL-1 and TNF-α. These cytokines act on nearby blood vessel endothelial cells, causing them to express adhesion molecules that let circulating neutrophils stick and squeeze into the tissue. Simultaneously, IL-8 (a chemokine) diffuses outward, forming a gradient that neutrophils follow toward the infection—higher chemokine concentration nearer the wound draws them in. Once there, IL-6 signals the liver to produce acute-phase proteins, and prostaglandin-cytokine crosstalk raises body temperature, producing fever. Each step is a distinct cytokine performing a distinct job, but together they produce the coordinated response we call inflammation.

Problem-solving application. Cytokine biology explains real clinical phenomena. In sepsis, uncontrolled release of TNF-α and IL-6—a "cytokine storm"—causes dangerously low blood pressure and organ damage, illustrating that a signal beneficial in small amounts becomes harmful in excess. This concept also underlies modern therapeutics: monoclonal antibodies like anti-TNF drugs (used for rheumatoid arthritis) work by blocking a specific cytokine's receptor binding, dampening inflammation without shutting down the entire immune system. If you're asked why blocking IL-6 receptors might reduce fever and joint swelling in an autoimmune patient, the answer traces back to IL-6's role in the inflammatory cascade above: interrupt the messenger, and the downstream cellular response—vessel permeability, immune cell recruitment, fever—weakens accordingly.
