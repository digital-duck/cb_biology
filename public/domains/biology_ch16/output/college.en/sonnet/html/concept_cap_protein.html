Cap Protein

Definition. CAP (catabolite activator protein, also called CRP, cyclic AMP receptor protein) is a positive transcriptional regulator in bacteria such as E. coli. It works only when bound to cyclic AMP (cAMP), a small signaling molecule whose intracellular concentration rises when glucose is scarce. The CAP–cAMP complex binds specific DNA sequences upstream of promoters that control sugar-processing operons (like the lac operon), where it makes direct contact with RNA polymerase and stabilizes its binding to the promoter. The result is increased transcription of genes needed to metabolize alternative sugars — but only when glucose, the cell's preferred energy source, is unavailable.

Worked example. Consider the lac operon, which encodes enzymes for digesting lactose. Its promoter is intrinsically weak — RNA polymerase binds it inefficiently on its own. When glucose is present, cAMP levels stay low, CAP remains inactive, and lac transcription stays minimal even if lactose is present. When glucose runs out, an enzyme called adenylate cyclase produces more cAMP, cAMP binds CAP, and the activated CAP–cAMP complex attaches to a site next to the lac promoter, helping RNA polymerase dock and initiate transcription far more efficiently. The lac operon is also controlled by a separate repressor protein that responds to lactose itself, but for this section, treat that repressor as already accounted for so we can focus purely on how CAP responds to glucose status. Under that simplification, CAP activation alone tracks one condition: is glucose absent?

Problem-solving application. This glucose-sensing logic explains a classic phenomenon called diauxic growth: when bacteria are given a glucose-lactose mixture, they consume glucose first, pause briefly, then resume growth on lactose only after glucose depletes and cAMP-CAP activation kicks in. If you're asked to predict how strongly CAP activates a sugar operon under two conditions — glucose present versus glucose absent — work through it systematically: with glucose present, cAMP stays low, so CAP cannot bind DNA or contact RNA polymerase, and activation is minimal regardless of what other sugars are available. With glucose absent, cAMP accumulates, CAP becomes active, and transcription of the target operon increases substantially. Notice that this single primitive — an activator protein that is only "on" when a small signaling molecule is present — recurs throughout bacterial gene regulation and even in eukaryotic systems (where cAMP-responsive proteins control pathways from hormone signaling to memory formation). Mastering how one signal (cAMP concentration) toggles one regulator (CAP) between active and inactive states gives you a template for reasoning about many two-state regulatory switches, even before you layer in additional controls like repressors.
