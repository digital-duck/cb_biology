Regulation Of Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is not a fixed assembly line running at constant speed — it is a demand-driven system. A cell converts glucose to ATP only as fast as it needs to, because overproducing ATP wastes glucose and underproducing it starves the cell of energy. This balance is achieved through two control points: transport of fuel into the cell and allosteric regulation of key enzymes within the pathway.

Glucose enters cells through transmembrane proteins called GLUT transporters. Some tissues (e.g., liver, pancreas) express GLUT2, which allows glucose entry proportional to blood glucose concentration, letting these tissues sense and respond to overall glucose levels. Muscle and fat cells use GLUT4, which is normally stored inside the cell and only inserted into the plasma membrane when insulin signals that glucose is abundant. This is the first checkpoint: no transporter, no fuel, no respiration.

Once inside, glucose catabolism is throttled by allosteric feedback at three rate-limiting enzymes: phosphofructokinase (glycolysis), pyruvate dehydrogenase (link reaction), and isocitrate dehydrogenase (citric acid cycle). Each is inhibited by high ATP and by NADH — signals that the cell already has ample energy — and activated by ADP and AMP, signals that ATP is being consumed faster than it is made. Citrate, an early product of the citric acid cycle, also inhibits phosphofructokinase, so a backlog in the cycle slows glycolysis upstream, preventing a pileup of intermediates.

Worked example. Suppose a resting muscle cell has a high ATP/ADP ratio and elevated NADH. ATP and NADH bind allosteric sites on phosphofructokinase and pyruvate dehydrogenase, reducing their activity. Flux through glycolysis and the citric acid cycle drops, oxygen consumption falls, and ATP production matches the low demand. When exercise begins, ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP and AMP; these bind the same enzymes' activating sites, relieving inhibition. Enzyme activity — and ATP output — rises within seconds, without needing new enzyme synthesis.

Application. This explains why measuring a cell's ADP/ATP ratio predicts its respiration rate better than measuring glucose availability alone: a cell can be swimming in glucose yet still respire slowly if its ATP demand is low, because allosteric inhibition, not fuel scarcity, is limiting flux.
