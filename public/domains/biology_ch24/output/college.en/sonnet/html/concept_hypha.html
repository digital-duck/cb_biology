Hypha

A hypha (plural: hyphae) is a single, thread-like filament of cytoplasm enclosed by a tubular cell wall, and it is the basic structural and functional unit of nearly all multicellular fungi. Hyphae grow only at their tips, elongating through the continuous synthesis and insertion of new cell wall material, and they branch repeatedly to form a spreading network called a mycelium. Internally, hyphae fall into two architectural types. Septate hyphae are divided into compartments by cross-walls called septa, which typically contain pores that allow cytoplasm, nutrients, and even organelles to flow between adjacent cells. Coenocytic (aseptate) hyphae lack these dividing walls entirely, so the filament is one continuous, multinucleate cytoplasmic mass. This structural distinction is a key trait used to classify fungal groups: for example, most members of the phylum Ascomycota have septate hyphae, while Zygomycota (bread molds like Rhizopus) are largely coenocytic.

Consider a fungus growing through soil in search of a buried food source, such as a decaying leaf. A single hypha extends by tip growth, secreting digestive enzymes ahead of itself and absorbing the resulting nutrients through its wall — the fungus's characteristic mode of "external digestion." As it grows, it branches into many hyphae, and this branching network (the mycelium) maximizes surface area for absorption while allowing the organism to explore a much larger volume of substrate than a single filament could.

The septate/coenocytic distinction has real problem-solving consequences. Suppose a fungal hypha is physically damaged or one section is walled off by the plant's defenses. In a septate hypha, the septal pores can be rapidly plugged (often by a protein structure called a Woronin body in ascomycetes), sealing off the injured compartment and preventing the rest of the mycelium from losing cytoplasm — damage stays localized. In a coenocytic hypha, there are no internal walls to seal, so an injury exposes the shared cytoplasm along the entire uninterrupted filament, making the whole structure more vulnerable to a single wound or invading pathogen. Understanding hyphal architecture, then, lets you predict how a fungal species will respond to injury, competition, or antifungal treatment — a working example of structure explaining physiological resilience.
