Mycosis

A mycosis is a disease caused by fungal infection of host tissue, resulting from the fungus colonizing, invading, and damaging cells rather than merely producing a toxin (as in mycotoxicosis) or triggering an allergic response. Mycoses are classified by the depth and extent of tissue involvement: superficial mycoses affect only the outermost layers of skin, hair, or nails; systemic (or invasive) mycoses spread through internal organs and the bloodstream; and opportunistic mycoses occur when normally harmless or environmental fungi exploit a weakened host, such as someone who is immunocompromised.

Worked example. Consider ringworm (tinea corporis), caused by dermatophyte fungi like Trichophyton. These fungi digest keratin, the structural protein in skin's outer layer, using secreted enzymes called keratinases. Because dermatophytes cannot survive at core body temperature or tolerate the immune defenses of living, deeper tissue, the infection stays confined to dead keratinized cells at the skin surface — this is why it is classified as superficial. Contrast this with Candida albicans, a yeast that lives harmlessly in the mouth, gut, and skin of most healthy people. In someone with an intact immune system, competing bacteria and immune surveillance keep its population in check. But in a patient undergoing chemotherapy, whose white blood cell count has collapsed, Candida can proliferate unchecked, invade the bloodstream, and reach organs like the kidneys or heart — becoming a systemic, opportunistic mycosis called candidemia, which can be fatal without antifungal treatment.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a hospital lab receives a sample from a patient with a skin lesion and needs to decide how urgently to act. Applying the classification framework, they would ask three diagnostic questions: (1) Is the infection confined to skin, hair, or nails, or has it penetrated to blood or organs? (2) Is the patient's immune system compromised (e.g., HIV, chemotherapy, organ transplant on immunosuppressants)? (3) Is the fungus a known aggressive pathogen or an opportunist? A localized rash in a healthy patient with no risk factors points toward a superficial mycosis, manageable with a topical antifungal cream. But the same rash-like symptoms in a neutropenic cancer patient warrant blood cultures and imaging, because opportunistic fungi can rapidly progress to systemic disease in such hosts. This triage logic — matching treatment urgency to depth of tissue involvement and host immune status — is exactly how clinicians prioritize antifungal therapy and infection-control decisions in practice.
