Microscope

A microscope is an instrument that magnifies objects too small to be seen with the naked eye, allowing scientists to observe structures such as cells, bacteria, and subcellular organelles. It works by using one or more lenses (or, in electron microscopes, focused beams of electrons) to bend light or particles in a way that produces an enlarged image of the specimen. The two properties that determine how useful a microscope is are magnification — how many times larger the image appears compared to the actual object — and resolution — the smallest distance between two points that can still be distinguished as separate. A microscope with high magnification but poor resolution produces a large, blurry image, which is far less useful than a smaller but sharp one.

Worked example. Suppose a compound light microscope has a 10x eyepiece and you switch between a 4x, 10x, and 40x objective lens. Total magnification is the product of the two: eyepiece magnification times objective magnification. At 4x objective, total magnification is $10 \times 4 = 40\times$; at 10x, it is $100\times$; at 40x (called "high power"), it is $400\times$. If a red blood cell has an actual diameter of about 7.5 micrometers ($\mu m$), then under 400x magnification, its image on your retina or on a projected screen appears $7.5 \times 400 = 3000\ \mu m$, or 3 millimeters, across — large enough to examine its shape and check for abnormalities like sickle-cell deformation.

Problem-solving application. In practice, biologists must choose the right magnification and lens combination for the task, not simply the highest one available. If you are asked to identify whether a water sample contains parasites roughly 50 $\mu m$ long, you would first scan at low power (say, 100x) to locate objects of the right general size and position them in the field of view, then switch to high power (400x or higher) to confirm identifying features. Jumping straight to the highest magnification narrows the field of view so much that you may never find the specimen at all. This two-step strategy — survey at low power, confirm at high power — is standard procedure in labs and reflects a broader problem-solving principle: match your tool's resolving power to the scale of the question you're actually asking, rather than defaulting to "more magnification is always better."
