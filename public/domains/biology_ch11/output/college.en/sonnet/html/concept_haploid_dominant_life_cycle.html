Haploid Dominant Life Cycle

Definition. In a haploid dominant life cycle, the organism that grows, feeds, and persists for most of its life is haploid — each cell carries a single set of chromosomes ($n$). Diploid tissue ($2n$) exists only briefly, as a zygote. As soon as two haploid gametes fuse to form that zygote, it undergoes meiosis rather than mitosis, immediately splitting back into haploid cells called spores. Those spores then grow, by mitosis, into the next haploid multicellular generation. This pattern dominates fungi and many algae, and contrasts sharply with animals, where the diploid stage is the long-lived body and haploid cells exist only as short-lived gametes (sperm and egg).

Worked example. Consider black bread mold (Rhizopus). The visible fuzzy mass growing on bread is haploid mycelium — thread-like haploid cells produced by ordinary mitotic growth. When two compatible haploid mycelia meet, their cells fuse, combining two haploid nuclei into one diploid zygospore. This zygospore is the only diploid stage in the entire life cycle, and it does not grow into a diploid mold. Instead, it undergoes meiosis right away, producing haploid spores. Each spore germinates and grows mitotically into a new haploid mycelium, restarting the cycle. Diploidy here is a single transitional event — a "genetic mixing" step — not a life stage.

Problem-solving application. This life-cycle logic explains a counterintuitive genetics fact: in haploid-dominant organisms, recessive mutations are expressed immediately, because there is no second, masking allele. A geneticist studying a fungal strain with a mutation affecting spore color can therefore observe the phenotype directly in the haploid spores after meiosis, without needing to cross multiple generations to reveal a recessive trait — unlike in diploid organisms such as humans, where a recessive allele can hide unexpressed for generations behind a dominant partner allele. This is also why fungi are classic tools in genetics labs: because meiosis immediately follows zygote formation, researchers can dissect the four (or eight) resulting spores from a single meiotic event and directly examine how alleles for different traits sorted and recombined — a technique called tetrad analysis, impossible to perform this cleanly in diploid-dominant organisms like ourselves.
