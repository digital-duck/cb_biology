Gametes

A gamete is a specialized haploid reproductive cell — an egg or a sperm — that fuses with another gamete during fertilization to produce a diploid zygote. "Haploid" means the cell carries a single set of chromosomes, denoted $n$, rather than the paired set found in ordinary body (somatic) cells, denoted $2n$. In humans, $n = 23$, so somatic cells carry $2n = 46$ chromosomes while gametes carry only 23. This halving is essential: if gametes were diploid like other cells, fertilization would double the chromosome number every generation. Instead, egg ($n$) plus sperm ($n$) restores the diploid number ($2n$) in the zygote, keeping the species' chromosome count stable across generations.

Gametes are produced by a specialized cell division called meiosis, which reduces a diploid parent cell to four haploid daughter cells. Meiosis also shuffles genetic material through crossing over and independent assortment, so no two gametes from the same individual are genetically identical — this is the primary source of genetic variation within a species.

Worked example. Suppose a fruit fly has a diploid chromosome number of $2n = 8$. How many chromosomes are in each of its gametes, and how many are restored in the zygote after fertilization? Each gamete carries $n = 4$ chromosomes. At fertilization, egg (4) and sperm (4) combine to form a zygote with $2n = 8$ chromosomes — matching the parent generation.

Problem-solving application. Gamete number problems appear frequently in genetics coursework and are solved with simple bookkeeping rather than formulas. Suppose a plant species has 12 chromosomes in its root cells. A student is asked: (1) how many chromosomes will a pollen grain (the plant's sperm-equivalent gamete) contain, and (2) if that plant self-fertilizes, how many chromosomes will the seed's embryo have? Since root cells are somatic (diploid), $2n = 12$, so $n = 6$ per gamete. Self-fertilization unites two gametes each with 6 chromosomes, restoring $2n = 12$ in the embryo. This reasoning — identify whether a cell is diploid or haploid, then halve or double accordingly — is the core skill tested in chromosome-counting problems across biology courses.
