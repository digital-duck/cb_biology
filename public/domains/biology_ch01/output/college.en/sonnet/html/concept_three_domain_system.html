Three Domain System

Before the 1970s, biologists sorted life into two great categories: prokaryotes (cells without a nucleus) and eukaryotes (cells with one). This scheme rested on visible cell structure. Carl Woese overturned it by comparing genetic sequences instead of anatomy — specifically, the sequence of the small subunit ribosomal RNA (16S/18S rRNA), a molecule present in every living cell because it is essential to building proteins. Because this molecule changes very slowly over evolutionary time, differences in its sequence between species serve as a molecular clock, revealing relationships invisible under a microscope. Woese's comparisons showed that what everyone had lumped together as "bacteria" actually split into two profoundly different lineages, giving three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. Archaea look like bacteria (no nucleus, single-celled) but are genetically as distant from bacteria as bacteria are from humans, and they share several molecular features — such as parts of their transcription machinery — with eukaryotes.

Worked example. Suppose you align a 16S rRNA sequence fragment from three organisms and count nucleotide differences per 100 bases: Organism A vs. B: 40 differences; A vs. C: 65 differences; B vs. C: 63 differences. Since A and B differ least, they are more closely related to each other than either is to C. This is exactly the logic Woese used at scale: he built a matrix of pairwise sequence differences across hundreds of organisms and found that the differences clustered into three distinct groups rather than two, with the gap between groups far exceeding the variation within any single group.

Problem-solving application. Given rRNA divergence data for an unclassified microorganism, you can assign it to a domain without ever examining it under a microscope. First, sequence its 16S rRNA gene. Then compare it against reference sequences from known Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. The domain with the smallest sequence divergence is the correct classification. This exact procedure is how scientists identified entire groups of extremophiles — organisms living in boiling hydrothermal vents or saturated brine — as Archaea, even though their cell shape and Gram-stain behavior looked bacterial. The lesson generalizes: when physical traits mislead, molecular sequence comparison provides an objective, quantifiable criterion for classification.
