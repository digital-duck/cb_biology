Organism

An organism is any individual living entity capable of carrying out the basic functions of life: acquiring and using energy (metabolism), maintaining internal stability (homeostasis), responding to stimuli, growing, and reproducing. Organisms range from single-celled bacteria to multicellular plants, fungi, and animals, but every one of them must solve the same underlying problem — extracting usable energy and matter from the environment and organizing it to sustain and replicate a highly ordered, non-equilibrium structure.

Worked example. Consider a single yeast cell placed in a sugar solution. It takes in glucose and oxygen, breaks the glucose down through cellular respiration to release usable energy (ATP), and uses that energy to synthesize proteins, repair its membrane, and eventually divide into two daughter cells. Each of the five defining functions is visible here: metabolism (glucose breakdown), homeostasis (regulating internal pH and ion concentration despite a changing external solution), response (adjusting metabolic rate if oxygen becomes scarce, shifting to fermentation), growth (increase in cell mass), and reproduction (budding into two cells). Notice that "organism" applies equally to this single cell and to a giant sequoia tree with trillions of cells — the defining criterion is functional autonomy as a living unit, not size or complexity.

Problem-solving application. Biologists often need to decide whether something counts as an organism versus a component of one, or versus a non-living entity. Apply the five-function test directly: A virus, for instance, cannot metabolize or reproduce on its own — it hijacks a host cell's machinery to copy itself — so by this definition, a virus is not classified as an organism, but a mitochondrion (which also cannot survive or reproduce independently outside a cell) is likewise excluded, despite once having been a free-living bacterium. Conversely, a coral colony made of thousands of interconnected polyps raises a harder case: each polyp can feed and respond independently, yet the colony shares a nervous and circulatory network, so ecologists sometimes treat it as one organism and sometimes as a colony of many, depending on the question being asked (e.g., "How many individuals died in this reef event?"). When you encounter an ambiguous case, the productive move is not to force a single label but to specify which functional criterion (independent metabolism, independent reproduction, or genetic individuality) is doing the classifying work for your particular problem.
