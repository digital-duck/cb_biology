Concept Book: Gel Electrophoresis

Biology Ch14 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Gel electrophoresis is where every prior concept in this book stops being abstract and starts producing a picture you can hold up to the light. A gel is nothing more than a molecular sieve: a slab of agarose or polyacrylamide riddled with microscopic pores. When an electric field is applied across it, charged molecules migrate through those pores toward the oppositely charged electrode. DNA and RNA, uniformly negatively charged along their sugar-phosphate backbone, move toward the positive electrode; the rate of migration depends almost entirely on size, because smaller fragments thread the pore network faster than larger ones. Run a mixture long enough and it resolves itself, unassisted, into discrete bands ordered by length. No other concept in this book pays off so directly: sequencing gives you a string of letters, restriction enzymes give you a specific cut, PCR gives you millions of copies of one region — but electrophoresis is what turns any of those outputs into a visible, measurable, comparable result. It is the natural endpoint because it is the universal readout.

Consider how it closes the loop on the applications this book has built toward. In diagnostic testing, PCR amplifies a suspected pathogen's DNA, and the gel confirms amplification actually occurred by showing a band of the expected size — no band, no infection detected. In paternity and forensic identification, short tandem repeat fragments of different lengths from two people are run side by side; matching band patterns are the literal evidence presented in court. In genetic engineering and cloning, after cutting a plasmid with a restriction enzyme, electrophoresis confirms the cut worked and isolates the fragment of interest by size before it is spliced into a new vector. In evolutionary and population studies, comparing band patterns across species or individuals reveals shared or divergent DNA fragments, turning an abstract question of relatedness into a side-by-side visual comparison. In every case, the same physical principle — charge, sieve, field — is repurposed to answer a completely different biological question.

Worked example: a lab runs a PCR product expected to be 500 base pairs alongside a DNA ladder with reference bands at 100, 300, 500, and 1000 bp. If the sample band aligns with the 500 bp reference, the amplification is confirmed as the correct target; a band at 300 bp instead would indicate nonspecific amplification, telling the researcher to redesign their primers.

From here, the richest next step is forensic DNA fingerprinting: explore how a handful of STR bands, run and compared this same way, became precise enough to identify a single person out of billions.
