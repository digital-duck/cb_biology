Flower

A flower is a specialized reproductive shoot found in angiosperms (flowering plants), composed of four whorls of modified leaves arranged around a shortened stem tip called the receptacle. From outside in, these whorls are the sepals (collectively the calyx, which protect the developing bud), the petals (collectively the corolla, often colorful to attract pollinators), the stamens (the male reproductive structures, each consisting of a filament and a pollen-producing anther), and the carpels (the female reproductive structures, together forming the pistil, which includes the stigma, style, and ovary). The ovary encloses one or more ovules, each containing an egg cell; after fertilization, the ovule becomes a seed and the ovary matures into a fruit. This arrangement — gametes protected inside tissue rather than exposed, as in the cones of gymnosperms — is a defining innovation of flowering plants and a major reason they dominate most terrestrial ecosystems today.

Worked example. Consider a lily flower, which has 3 sepals, 3 petals, 6 stamens, and a pistil made of 3 fused carpels. If you dissect the flower and count 6 anthers, you know pollen is being produced at 6 separate sites. If you then slice the ovary crosswise and count 3 internal chambers (locules), this confirms the pistil is compound, formed from 3 carpels fused together rather than 3 separate pistils. This kind of structural count — sepals, petals, stamens, carpels — is exactly how botanists classify plant families; the "floral formula" for a lily is written as 3 sepals + 3 petals + 6 stamens + 3 fused carpels, and comparing this formula across species reveals evolutionary relationships.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you're given an unlabeled flower and asked to determine whether it can self-pollinate. You would examine whether stamens and carpels are both present (a "perfect" flower) and whether the anthers release pollen before, during, or after the stigma of the same flower becomes receptive. If stamens mature and shed pollen while the stigma is still immature (a timing mismatch called protandry), self-pollination within that flower is prevented even though both organs are present — forcing you to look for cross-pollination instead. This kind of reasoning — mapping structure to function and function to reproductive outcome — is the core skill flowers are used to teach: predicting a plant's breeding strategy directly from its floral anatomy.
