Pollination

Pollination is the transfer of pollen — the structure carrying a plant's male gametes — from an anther (in flowering plants) or a male cone (in conifers and other gymnosperms) to a receptive female structure: a stigma in flowers, or the ovule-bearing scale of a female cone. Landing there is only the first step. The pollen grain must then germinate, growing a pollen tube that tunnels through female tissue toward the ovule, delivering sperm nuclei to fertilize the egg. Pollination and fertilization are therefore distinct events: pollination is delivery, fertilization is the actual fusion of gametes, and the pollen tube is the bridge between them.

Worked example. Consider a bee visiting a flower. Pollen grains stick to the bee's body while it feeds on nectar, then rub off onto the stigma of the next flower it visits — this is biotic (animal-mediated) pollination. Once a compatible grain lands on the stigma, it absorbs moisture and nutrients from the stigma's surface, triggering germination: the grain extrudes a pollen tube that grows down through the style, guided chemically toward an ovule in the ovary. Wind-pollinated plants like grasses and pines skip the animal intermediary entirely, releasing enormous quantities of lightweight pollen so that, by sheer statistical odds, some grains reach a receptive stigma or cone scale.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you are advising a farmer whose almond orchard is producing far fewer nuts than expected, even though the trees flower heavily and look healthy. Because almonds require insect pollination and most cultivars are self-incompatible, you would systematically rule out causes tied to pollen transfer rather than plant health: Are compatible pollinizer varieties planted nearby? Is bee activity sufficient during the narrow bloom window? Were pesticides applied during flowering, killing pollinators? Was cold, wet weather suppressing bee flight when flowers were receptive? This diagnostic approach — treating low yield as a pollination-transfer problem rather than a growth or nutrition problem — is exactly how agronomists troubleshoot fruit-set failures, and it generalizes to any pollination-dependent crop, from apples to squash.
