Cell Growth Response

Definition. When a growth factor—such as epidermal growth factor (EGF) or platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF)—binds to its matching receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) on the cell surface, it triggers one of the most consequential decisions a cell can make: whether to divide. Binding causes two RTK molecules to pair up, switching on their internal kinase activity. Each receptor then attaches phosphate groups to the other, and these tagged sites recruit a chain of relay proteins inside the cell. That relay—known as the RAS/MAP kinase cascade—passes the "divide" signal step by step until it reaches the nucleus, where it switches on the genes needed to move the cell through its growth and division cycle. The core idea to hold onto is simple: a signal received at the membrane is relayed, protein by protein, into a change in gene activity.

Worked example. Picture a skin wound. Platelets at the injury release PDGF, which diffuses to nearby fibroblasts and binds RTKs on their surface. This triggers the relay described above, ending with the activation of genes that push the fibroblasts to proliferate and migrate into the wound, laying down new tissue. Once healing is complete, PDGF is no longer released: with no ligand to bind the receptor, the relay never starts, and division stops.

Problem-solving application. Because this pathway is a chain of discrete steps, it becomes a tool for predicting cell behavior when any single step malfunctions. Suppose a tumor biopsy shows a mutation that locks the relay in the "on" position partway along the chain. Even with no growth factor present, the signal would still reach the nucleus and drive continuous division—the logic behind many cancers. A drug that blocks the relay at or after that mutated step can still shut the signal down, even though blocking the receptor itself would do nothing, since the problem lies downstream of it. Conversely, if the receptor gene itself is deleted, no amount of growth factor produces a response, because the relay never begins. By asking "where in the relay is the signal stuck, and where is it blocked?", researchers can predict outcomes and choose interventions that target the right point in the chain—turning a diagram of protein interactions into a practical diagnostic and therapeutic tool.
