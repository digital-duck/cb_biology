Dehydration Synthesis

Definition. Dehydration synthesis is a chemical reaction in which two smaller molecules, called monomers, combine to form a larger molecule, a polymer, by creating a new covalent bond between them. The reaction gets its name from what is removed in the process: for every bond formed, one water molecule ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is released. One monomer contributes a hydroxyl group (–OH), and the other contributes a hydrogen atom (–H); these combine to form the departing water, while the atoms left behind on each monomer bond directly to one another. This reaction is the fundamental mechanism cells use to build the macromolecules of life — proteins, carbohydrates, nucleic acids, and lipids — from their individual building blocks.

Worked example. Consider two glucose molecules ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) joining to form the disaccharide maltose. Each glucose molecule has multiple hydroxyl groups. During dehydration synthesis, the –OH group on carbon 1 of one glucose reacts with the –H from the hydroxyl group on carbon 4 of the second glucose. A covalent bond (called a glycosidic bond) forms between the two carbons, and the leftover –OH and –H combine to form one water molecule:

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow \text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Notice that the product, maltose, has exactly two fewer hydrogen atoms and one fewer oxygen atom than the sum of the two glucose molecules — accounting precisely for the water lost. This same pattern — lose one $\text{H}$, one $\text{OH}$, gain one bond and one $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ — applies whether you are linking amino acids into a protein via a peptide bond, nucleotides into DNA via a phosphodiester bond, or fatty acids to glycerol to form a fat.

Problem-solving application. Because the stoichiometry is so regular, you can predict molecular formulas for polymers without memorizing each reaction. If $n$ glucose monomers join into a single starch chain, they form $n-1$ bonds — not $n$, since a chain of $n$ units has only $n-1$ connections between them — releasing $n-1$ water molecules along the way. For example, a starch chain of 500 glucose units releases 499 water molecules, a count you can verify by comparing the total atoms in 500 separate glucose molecules to the atoms actually present in the final polysaccharide chain: the difference is exactly 499 water molecules' worth of hydrogen and oxygen. The same reasoning generalizes directly to any monomer-polymer system, including proteins built from amino acids and nucleic acids built from nucleotides — count the bonds in the finished chain, and that number tells you how many water molecules were released to build it.
