Pharmacogenomics

Pharmacogenomics studies how variations in an individual's genome affect their response to drugs — how quickly they metabolize a medication, whether it will work at the prescribed dose, and whether it is likely to cause serious side effects. The related term toxicogenomics emphasizes the safety side of this same question: predicting adverse or toxic reactions from genomic data before a drug is ever administered. The core insight is that "one dose fits all" is a statistical convenience, not a biological reality — genes encoding drug-metabolizing enzymes, transporters, and drug targets vary from person to person, and that variation changes outcomes.

Worked example. Consider the gene CYP2D6, which encodes an enzyme responsible for metabolizing roughly a quarter of all prescribed drugs, including codeine. Codeine itself is inactive; the liver must convert it into morphine to produce pain relief. People with extra copies of a fully functional CYP2D6 gene are "ultra-rapid metabolizers": they convert codeine to morphine unusually fast, and even a standard dose can produce morphine levels high enough to cause dangerous respiratory depression, an effect documented in nursing infants of breastfeeding mothers prescribed codeine. At the other extreme, "poor metabolizers" barely convert codeine at all, so the drug provides little pain relief no matter the dose. Genomic testing for CYP2D6 variants before prescribing lets a physician choose a different analgesic or adjust the dose, rather than discovering the mismatch after an adverse event.

Problem-solving application. In practice, clinicians and pharmacists use pharmacogenomic testing to answer three linked questions for any candidate drug: Will it work in this patient (efficacy)? At what dose (dosing)? And what is the risk of harm (toxicity)? For warfarin, an anticoagulant with a narrow safety margin, variants in CYP2C9 and VKORC1 are combined with age, weight, and other clinical variables in validated dosing algorithms to estimate a starting dose far more accurately than trial-and-error titration. The broader lesson generalizes beyond any single gene: whenever a treatment's benefit-to-harm ratio depends sensitively on individual biology, genomic information can be incorporated as one more input variable into a decision rule, shifting medicine from population averages toward individualized risk-benefit calculations.
