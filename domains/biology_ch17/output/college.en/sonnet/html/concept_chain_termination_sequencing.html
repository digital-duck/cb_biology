Chain Termination Sequencing

Determining the order of nucleotides in a DNA molecule requires a way to stop replication at a known position for every possible position along the strand, then sort the resulting fragments by length. Chain termination sequencing, developed by Frederick Sanger in 1977, solves this using dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs) — modified nucleotides that lack the 3'-hydroxyl group needed to attach the next base. When DNA polymerase incorporates a ddNTP instead of a normal deoxynucleotide (dNTP), synthesis of that strand stops permanently.

A sequencing reaction combines the single-stranded template, a primer, DNA polymerase, an abundant supply of normal dNTPs, and a small amount of fluorescently labeled ddNTPs (one color per base: A, T, C, G). As polymerase extends the primer, most nucleotides added are normal dNTPs, but occasionally — randomly, at every position — a ddNTP gets incorporated instead, terminating that particular copy of the strand. Because millions of template copies are being synthesized in parallel, termination events occur at every possible position across the population, generating a complete set of fragments of every length, each ending in a color-coded ddNTP that identifies its final base.

These fragments are separated by capillary gel electrophoresis, which sorts DNA fragments by size: shorter fragments migrate faster through the gel matrix, longer ones lag behind. A laser detector reads the fluorescent label on each fragment as it passes, in order from shortest to longest. Since each length corresponds to a specific position in the original sequence, and each terminating ddNTP identifies the base at that position, reading the colors in order of increasing fragment length reconstructs the original sequence base by base.

Consider a template requiring sequencing of a 10-base region. The reaction produces fragments terminating at position 1 through position 10, one color per position. If electrophoresis resolves these ten fragments by length and the detector reads colors corresponding to A-T-G-C-C-A-T-G-A-T in order of increasing size, that is the sequence of the template's complementary strand — from which the original strand's sequence follows immediately by base-pairing rules.

This method's main limitation is read length: resolution of fragment sizes on a gel or capillary becomes unreliable beyond about 800–1000 bases, since fragments differing by one nucleotide near that length are too similar to separate cleanly. This constraint motivated the shotgun sequencing strategies and, later, next-generation sequencing technologies used for whole-genome projects.
