Amino Acid Derived Hormone

Not all hormones are proteins or steroids. A third class is built from single amino acids that have been chemically modified — usually by adding or removing small groups — to become signaling molecules. These are amino acid–derived hormones, and their two most familiar examples, epinephrine and melatonin, come from different starting amino acids and act in very different ways, which makes them a useful pair for understanding the category.

Epinephrine (adrenaline) is derived from tyrosine through a short enzymatic pathway that also produces norepinephrine and dopamine — collectively called catecholamines. Melatonin is derived from tryptophan, the same amino acid that gives rise to serotonin. Because these hormones are small and structurally similar to their parent amino acids, they are typically water-soluble. This matters for how they work: water-soluble hormones cannot cross the lipid bilayer of a target cell, so they bind receptors on the cell surface, triggering a second-messenger cascade inside the cell rather than entering the nucleus directly (as steroid hormones do).

Worked example: Consider epinephrine released by the adrenal medulla during a stress response. It binds beta-adrenergic receptors on liver cells, activating an intracellular pathway (via cyclic AMP) that turns on the enzyme glycogen phosphorylase within seconds. The result — rapid glucose release into the blood — happens far faster than a steroid hormone's genomic mechanism could achieve, because no gene transcription is required.

Problem-solving application: Suppose a drug blocks tyrosine hydroxylase, the first enzyme in the tyrosine-to-catecholamine pathway. Predict the physiological consequence. Since tyrosine hydroxylase is the rate-limiting step for making dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine, blocking it would suppress production of all three — impairing the sympathetic "fight-or-flight" response (reduced heart rate and blood pressure elevation under stress) and potentially causing dopamine-related deficits, since dopamine is also a precursor in this same pathway. This kind of reasoning — tracing a shared biosynthetic pathway to predict downstream effects of blocking one enzyme — is exactly how pharmacologists evaluate drug targets, and it illustrates why classifying a hormone by its precursor amino acid is not just a naming convention: it tells you which biosynthetic pathway, and which other molecules, will be affected by any intervention at that pathway's entry point.
