Peptide Hormone

A peptide hormone is a signaling molecule built from a chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds, synthesized on ribosomes and secreted by endocrine cells into the bloodstream. Because peptide chains carry many polar and charged side groups, these hormones are water-soluble. This solubility dictates their entire mechanism of action: they cannot cross the lipid bilayer of a target cell's plasma membrane, so they must bind receptors on the cell surface, triggering an intracellular signaling cascade (a "second messenger" system) rather than entering the cell directly. This contrasts with steroid hormones, which are lipid-derived, cross membranes freely, and bind receptors inside the cell.

Worked example. Insulin, secreted by pancreatic beta cells, is a peptide hormone composed of two chains (51 amino acids total) joined by disulfide bonds. When blood glucose rises after a meal, insulin is released and binds to insulin receptors embedded in the membranes of liver, muscle, and fat cells. Binding activates a tyrosine kinase cascade inside the cell, ultimately causing glucose transporter proteins (GLUT4) to move to the cell surface and pull glucose out of the blood. Note the logic: insulin itself never enters the cell — the message is relayed by a chain of intracellular proteins, and the whole process can happen within seconds to minutes because no gene transcription is required for the immediate effect.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a patient has normal insulin levels but persistently high blood glucose. Two hypotheses could explain this: (1) the pancreas is producing insulin, but insulin receptors on target cells are unresponsive (as in Type 2 diabetes), or (2) something is degrading insulin before it reaches its receptor. To distinguish these experimentally, you could measure insulin receptor binding directly using labeled insulin on isolated cells: if labeled insulin binds normally but glucose uptake still fails, the defect lies downstream in the signaling cascade, not at the receptor itself. This kind of reasoning — tracing a signal from secretion, to receptor binding, to intracellular response — is the general strategy for diagnosing any peptide hormone disorder, since the water-soluble, receptor-mediated mechanism means the fault must lie in one of exactly three stages: production, receptor binding, or downstream signal transduction.
