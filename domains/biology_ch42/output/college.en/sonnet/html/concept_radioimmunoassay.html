Radioimmunoassay

Radioimmunoassay (RIA) is a laboratory technique that measures extremely small concentrations of a substance—hormones, drugs, vitamins, or viral proteins—by exploiting the specificity of antibody-antigen binding combined with the sensitivity of radioactive detection. The method works on a principle called competitive binding: a fixed, limited amount of antibody is mixed with a fixed amount of radioactively labeled antigen (the "tracer") and a variable amount of unlabeled antigen from the patient's sample. Because antibody binding sites are limited, labeled and unlabeled antigen molecules compete for the same sites. The more unlabeled antigen present in the sample, the more labeled tracer gets displaced and remains unbound, free in solution.

Worked example. Suppose a lab wants to measure insulin levels in a blood sample. They add a known quantity of radioactively tagged insulin and a fixed amount of anti-insulin antibody to several reference tubes, each spiked with a known concentration of unlabeled insulin (0, 10, 50, 100, 500 ng/mL). After the binding reaction reaches equilibrium, the bound and free fractions are physically separated (e.g., using a precipitating agent), and the radioactivity in the bound fraction is measured for each tube. Plotting percent of tracer bound against the known insulin concentration produces a standard curve that decreases monotonically—more competing unlabeled insulin means less tracer stays bound. The patient's sample is then run through the identical protocol, its bound-tracer signal is measured, and that signal is located on the standard curve to read off the corresponding insulin concentration.

Problem-solving application. RIA's power lies in detecting substances present at picogram-per-milliliter concentrations—far below what direct chemical assays can resolve—making it historically essential for measuring hormones like thyroid-stimulating hormone or reproductive hormones, whose normal physiological ranges are minuscule. When applying this technique, a key troubleshooting skill is recognizing sources of error: cross-reactivity (the antibody binding a structurally similar but distinct molecule, producing false elevation), and inadequate separation of bound versus free tracer, which distorts the standard curve. Understanding the competitive-binding logic also clarifies why RIA has been progressively replaced in many clinical settings by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), which uses enzymatic color change instead of radioactivity, avoiding the safety, waste-disposal, and shelf-life challenges of radioactive isotopes while preserving the same underlying antigen-antibody specificity.
