Hypersensitivity

Hypersensitivity is an exaggerated or misdirected immune response that causes tissue damage rather than protection. It always requires prior sensitization—a first exposure that primes the immune system—so symptoms appear on re-exposure, not the initial encounter. Immunologists classify hypersensitivity into four types (I–IV) based on the mechanism involved: Type I is IgE-mediated (immediate allergic reactions like hay fever or anaphylaxis); Type II involves IgG/IgM antibodies attacking cell-surface antigens (e.g., transfusion reactions); Type III involves immune complexes depositing in tissues (e.g., serum sickness, some autoimmune conditions); Type IV is cell-mediated, driven by T cells rather than antibodies, and is delayed—appearing 24–72 hours after exposure (e.g., poison ivy contact dermatitis, tuberculin skin test).

Worked example. Consider a person stung by a bee for the first time. No reaction beyond local swelling occurs, but their immune system generates IgE antibodies specific to bee venom proteins, which bind to mast cells throughout the body—this is sensitization. On a second sting, venom antigens cross-link the mast-cell-bound IgE, triggering rapid degranulation: histamine and other mediators flood the tissue, causing the vessel dilation, swelling, and potentially systemic anaphylaxis characteristic of Type I hypersensitivity. The key diagnostic clue is timing—minutes, not days—and the involvement of IgE and mast cells rather than T cells.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a patient develops an itchy, blistering rash 48 hours after wearing a new metal watch, but shows no reaction to a drug injected 10 minutes earlier despite having taken it before. How would you classify each event? The rash's delayed onset and lack of antibody involvement points to Type IV (T-cell-mediated) contact hypersensitivity to nickel—T cells recruited to the skin take time to mobilize and release inflammatory cytokines. If the drug reaction instead appeared within minutes as hives and throat tightness, that pattern indicates Type I. Clinically, distinguishing these types matters enormously: Type I is treated with antihistamines/epinephrine and prevented by allergen avoidance, while Type IV is managed with topical corticosteroids and avoidance of the specific contact allergen. Recognizing the time course, the antibody or cell type involved, and the tissue pattern is the core diagnostic skill for working through any hypersensitivity case.
