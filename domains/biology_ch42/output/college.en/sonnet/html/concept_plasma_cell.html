Plasma Cell

Definition. A plasma cell is a terminally differentiated B lymphocyte whose job is to manufacture and secrete antibodies (immunoglobulins) at high volume. It arises when a naive or memory B cell encounters an antigen that fits its surface receptor, then receives a second signal from a helper T cell — specifically, a TH2 cell delivering cytokines such as interleukin-4, IL-5, and IL-6. This two-signal requirement (antigen binding plus T-cell help) prevents B cells from activating against harmless or self molecules; without co-stimulation, an antigen-bound B cell typically becomes anergic (functionally silenced) rather than activated. Once fully differentiated, a plasma cell largely abandons the machinery a B cell used for antigen recognition and instead fills its cytoplasm with rough endoplasmic reticulum, becoming an antibody factory capable of secreting thousands of antibody molecules per second, though it lives only days to a few weeks.

Worked example. Consider a person exposed to a flu virus for the first time. A B cell whose receptor happens to match a viral surface protein binds the antigen and internalizes it, then displays a fragment on its MHC II molecule. A TH2 cell that recognizes this same fragment binds the B cell and releases IL-4 and IL-5. This combined signal drives the B cell to proliferate and differentiate: most daughter cells become plasma cells producing virus-specific antibodies, while a smaller subset becomes long-lived memory B cells. The antibodies then circulate and neutralize the virus or mark it for destruction by other immune cells.

Problem-solving application. This mechanism explains a puzzle in vaccine design: why do purified polysaccharide antigens (like some bacterial capsule vaccines) often produce weaker, shorter-lived immunity than protein-based ones? Polysaccharides cannot be presented on MHC II, so they cannot recruit TH2 help — B cells respond to them in a "T-independent" fashion, producing plasma cells but few memory cells and no affinity maturation. The engineering fix is a conjugate vaccine: chemically attaching the polysaccharide to a carrier protein. Now a B cell that binds the polysaccharide can internalize and present the attached protein fragment, recruiting TH2 help and triggering robust plasma cell output plus durable memory — the strategy behind vaccines like Hib and pneumococcal conjugate vaccines.
