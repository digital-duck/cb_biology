Epitope

An epitope is the specific molecular region of an antigen that an antibody, B cell receptor, or T cell receptor actually binds. Antigens — whether a viral protein, a pollen grain protein, or a bacterial toxin — are typically far too large for any single immune receptor to grip in their entirety. Instead, the immune system recognizes small, distinct patches on the antigen's surface, usually five to fifteen amino acids for a protein antigen. Each such patch is an epitope, and a single antigen commonly displays several different epitopes, each capable of being recognized by a different antibody or receptor.

Epitopes come in two structural types. A linear (sequential) epitope is a continuous stretch of amino acids along the protein chain; it can often be recognized even if the protein is denatured, because the sequence itself is the signature. A conformational epitope, by contrast, is built from amino acids that are far apart in the linear sequence but are brought into proximity by the way the protein folds in three dimensions; if the protein unfolds, this epitope is destroyed even though every amino acid is still present. This distinction matters practically: a diagnostic test using a short synthetic peptide will only detect antibodies against linear epitopes, missing any response directed at conformational ones.

Consider a worked example from vaccine design. The spike protein of a virus is the antigen. Researchers scanning it for good vaccine targets look for epitopes that are (a) accessible on the exposed surface of the folded protein, (b) conserved across viral strains, and (c) capable of provoking a strong antibody or T cell response. A region buried inside the protein's core, even if unique to that virus, is a poor epitope target because antibodies cannot reach it in the intact virus.

This generates a real design problem: given a candidate protein sequence and known 3D structure data, how would you predict which stretches are likely epitopes? The applied strategy is to combine three data types — surface accessibility scores (is the region exposed?), sequence variability across known strains (is it conserved?), and known binding assay data (has an antibody already been shown to bind there?) — and rank candidate regions accordingly. This same logic underlies peptide-based vaccine design, monoclonal antibody development, and the interpretation of ELISA and Western blot diagnostic results.
