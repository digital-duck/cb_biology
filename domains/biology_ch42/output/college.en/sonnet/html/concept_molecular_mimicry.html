Molecular Mimicry

Definition. Molecular mimicry occurs when the adaptive immune system, having generated antibodies or T cell receptors (TCRs) against a pathogen's antigens, produces receptors that also bind a structurally or chemically similar antigen on the host's own tissues. Because the immune system recognizes three-dimensional shape and chemical surface properties rather than the identity or origin of a molecule, a receptor optimized to grip a viral or bacterial protein can, by coincidence, fit a human protein that happens to share key contact residues. The result is an autoimmune response: immune cells trained to destroy an invader begin attacking healthy tissue that merely looks similar to it.

Worked example. The clearest clinical case is rheumatic fever following untreated Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A strep) throat infection. The bacterium's M protein shares epitopes with proteins in human cardiac muscle, particularly myosin, as well as with tissue in joints and the brain. Antibodies produced to clear the strep infection cross-react with these cardiac proteins, triggering inflammation in heart valves — a condition called rheumatic heart disease. A similar logic underlies hypotheses linking Campylobacter jejuni infection to Guillain-Barré syndrome, where bacterial lipooligosaccharides resemble gangliosides on peripheral nerve myelin, and antibody attack on the bacterium spills over onto nerve sheaths, causing ascending paralysis.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you are a clinician evaluating a patient with a recent streptococcal throat infection who now presents with joint pain and a new heart murmur. Molecular mimicry gives you a testable causal chain: pathogen exposure → antibody generation → cross-reactivity with self-antigen → tissue-specific damage. This chain suggests two practical interventions. First, prevention: treating strep throat promptly with antibiotics reduces antigen load before a robust cross-reactive antibody response can fully develop, which is why penicillin prophylaxis is standard practice in populations at risk for rheumatic fever. Second, diagnosis: because the self-antigen and pathogen antigen are structurally related but not identical, cross-reactive antibodies can sometimes be distinguished from purely anti-pathogen antibodies using assays with purified human tissue proteins, helping confirm autoimmune involvement rather than ongoing infection alone. More broadly, when you encounter an autoimmune disease with an unclear origin, molecular mimicry is one hypothesis worth testing: search for an antecedent infection and ask whether any of the pathogen's surface antigens share sequence or structural homology with the tissue under attack.
