Allergy

An allergy is a form of immediate hypersensitivity: an immune response that misfires against a harmless substance—pollen, peanut protein, cat dander—as though it were a dangerous pathogen. The reaction is mediated by immunoglobulin E (IgE), an antibody class normally deployed against parasites. On first exposure to an allergen, the immune system produces IgE antibodies specific to that substance. These antibodies attach to mast cells (found in skin and mucous membranes) and basophils (circulating in blood), essentially arming them like loaded triggers. On a later exposure, the allergen binds to this IgE, causing the mast cells to rapidly release histamine and other inflammatory chemicals. Because the cells are already primed, the reaction unfolds within minutes—hence "immediate" hypersensitivity, as opposed to delayed reactions (like poison ivy rashes) that take a day or more and use different immune cells.

Consider a person with a peanut allergy who accidentally eats a cookie containing peanut flour. Within minutes, histamine release causes blood vessels to dilate and become leaky, producing swelling, hives, and a drop in blood pressure; airway smooth muscle constricts, causing wheezing and difficulty breathing. In severe cases this cascade becomes anaphylaxis, a life-threatening, whole-body reaction. Epinephrine (adrenaline) is the emergency treatment because it directly counteracts these effects: it constricts blood vessels (raising blood pressure), relaxes airway muscle (easing breathing), and reduces further histamine release.

Understanding this mechanism lets you reason through both diagnosis and treatment logically rather than by memorization. Skin-prick allergy tests work by introducing a tiny amount of suspected allergen into the skin and watching for a localized histamine-driven wheal-and-flare reaction—a miniature, controlled version of the same IgE pathway. Antihistamine medications (like diphenhydramine) treat mild allergy symptoms by blocking histamine receptors after release, which is why they help with itching and sneezing but are too slow and too weak to reverse anaphylaxis once it starts. This is also why allergists recommend carrying an epinephrine auto-injector rather than relying on antihistamines alone for severe allergies: the two drugs intervene at different points in the same pathway, and only epinephrine acts fast enough and broadly enough to reverse a full anaphylactic cascade.
