Male Reproductive Anatomy

The male reproductive system is organized around a single functional goal: producing sperm cells, keeping them viable, and delivering them to the reproductive tract of a partner. Its structures fall into three categories — production, storage/transport, and delivery — and understanding the system means tracing the path a sperm cell actually travels.

Production begins in the testes, two oval organs suspended outside the body cavity in the scrotum. This external location matters functionally: spermatogenesis (sperm production) requires a temperature about 2–3°C below core body temperature, and the scrotum's muscles (the cremaster and dartos) contract or relax to pull the testes closer to or farther from the body, actively regulating this temperature. Inside each testis, sperm are produced in tightly coiled seminiferous tubules, while neighboring interstitial (Leydig) cells secrete testosterone.

Storage and maturation happen next. Newly made sperm are immotile and travel to the epididymis, a long coiled tube resting on each testis, where over roughly two weeks they mature and gain the ability to swim. During ejaculation, sperm move from the epididymis through the vas deferens (ductus deferens), a muscular tube that propels sperm forward via peristaltic contraction — the same wave-like muscular action found in the digestive tract.

Delivery requires more than sperm alone. Three glands — the seminal vesicles, prostate gland, and bulbourethral glands — add fluids as sperm pass through the ejaculatory duct into the urethra. This mixture, semen, provides fructose for energy, buffers to protect sperm from vaginal acidity, and lubrication. The urethra, which runs through the penis, serves a dual role, carrying either urine or semen, but never both simultaneously — a valve at the bladder's base closes during arousal to prevent mixing.

Applying the anatomy: consider a clinical scenario. A vasectomy severs and seals the vas deferens on each side. Sperm production in the testes and fluid production by the accessory glands continue unaffected — so ejaculate volume barely changes — but sperm can no longer reach the urethra and are instead reabsorbed by the body. This illustrates why tracing the pathway (testis → epididymis → vas deferens → ejaculatory duct → urethra) is more useful than memorizing structures in isolation: a fertility question is really a question of where along that pathway a blockage or intervention occurs.
