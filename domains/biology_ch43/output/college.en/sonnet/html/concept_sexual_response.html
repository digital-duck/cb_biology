Sexual Response

Human sexual response is a physiological and psychological sequence describing how the body reacts during sexual activity. Researchers William Masters and Virginia Johnson, working in the 1960s, organized this sequence into four phases: excitement, plateau, orgasm, and resolution. Their model remains a standard reference in physiology and health education because it links observable bodily changes—heart rate, blood flow, muscle tension—to a predictable, though variable, timeline.

During excitement, the body responds to physical or psychological stimulation. Heart rate and blood pressure rise, breathing quickens, and blood flow increases to the genitals, producing vasocongestion (tissue swelling due to blood pooling) and, in general, lubrication. This phase can last from minutes to hours and vary widely between individuals and situations.

The plateau phase intensifies the changes from excitement. Muscle tension increases throughout the body, heart rate and breathing continue to rise, and the body approaches a physiological threshold. This phase functions like a sustained buildup rather than a sharp transition—the body is primed but has not yet reached peak arousal.

Orgasm is the shortest phase, typically lasting only seconds, and involves involuntary muscle contractions in the pelvic region along with a peak release of physiological tension. It is accompanied by the release of neurochemicals such as oxytocin and endorphins, which contribute to sensations of pleasure and bonding.

Resolution is the body's return to its resting state. Heart rate, blood pressure, and breathing gradually normalize, and swollen tissues return to their unaroused state. Resolution time varies by individual and by biological sex; some bodies enter a refractory period during which renewed arousal is temporarily difficult.

Applying the model: Health professionals use this framework diagnostically. For example, if a patient reports difficulty reaching orgasm despite normal excitement and plateau responses, a clinician can localize the issue to a specific phase rather than treating "sexual difficulty" as one undifferentiated problem. Similarly, sex educators use the model to explain why arousal and satisfaction depend on adequate time in the plateau phase—rushing this stage is a common, treatable cause of dissatisfaction. The four-phase structure thus turns a subjective experience into a framework that supports both clinical diagnosis and practical communication between partners.
