Blood Glucose Regulation

Blood glucose regulation is the physiological negative feedback system that keeps blood sugar concentration near a set point, typically around 90 mg/dL (5 mM) in humans, using two antagonistic hormones from the pancreas: insulin and glucagon. When glucose rises above the set point, insulin lowers it; when glucose falls below the set point, glucagon raises it. Both hormones act on the same control loop but push in opposite directions, so the system behaves like a thermostat with a heater and an air conditioner rather than a single switch.

Worked example. After a carbohydrate-rich meal, blood glucose rises from 90 to 140 mg/dL. Specialized beta cells in the pancreatic islets detect this deviation and secrete insulin into the bloodstream. Insulin signals liver, muscle, and fat cells to take up glucose and store it as glycogen or fat, which drives blood glucose back down toward 90 mg/dL. As glucose approaches the set point, insulin secretion tapers off — this is the "negative" part of the feedback: the hormonal response counteracts, rather than amplifies, the original disturbance. Conversely, several hours later, between meals or during exercise, glucose drops to 70 mg/dL. Alpha cells in the pancreas detect this drop and secrete glucagon, which signals the liver to break down stored glycogen and release glucose into the blood, restoring the set point from below. The two hormones therefore define a narrow homeostatic band, not a single fixed value, and the loop continuously overshoots and corrects in small increments rather than settling instantly.

Problem-solving application. This model becomes clinically useful when the feedback loop is broken. In type 1 diabetes, beta cells fail to produce insulin, so the "rising glucose → insulin release" arm of the loop is missing; the corrective step must be supplied externally through injected insulin, timed and dosed to substitute for the missing sensor-response link. In type 2 diabetes, insulin is produced but target cells respond weakly to it — the sensor works, but the effector is desensitized, requiring higher insulin levels or medications that improve cellular sensitivity. Given a patient's glucose-time graph, you can diagnose which arm of the loop is disrupted by asking: does insulin get released at all, and if so, does glucose actually decline in response? This same logic — separating sensor, integrator, and effector failures — generalizes to diagnosing any homeostatic control system, from thyroid hormone regulation to thermoregulation.
