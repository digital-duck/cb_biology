Tonicity

Tonicity describes how a solution's solute concentration compares to that of a cell, and it predicts the net direction water will move across the cell's membrane by osmosis. A solution can be isotonic (equal solute concentration to the cell's interior), hypotonic (lower solute concentration than the cell), or hypertonic (higher solute concentration than the cell). Because water moves from regions of lower solute concentration to regions of higher solute concentration, tonicity determines whether a cell stays the same size, swells, or shrinks.

Worked example. Suppose a red blood cell, with an internal solute concentration of about 0.9% salt, is placed in three different solutions: (1) pure water, (2) a 0.9% saline solution, and (3) a 10% saline solution. In case 1, the surrounding solution is hypotonic relative to the cell — water is more concentrated outside than inside — so water flows into the cell, causing it to swell and potentially burst (lysis). In case 2, the solution is isotonic; water moves in and out at equal rates, and the cell maintains its normal shape. In case 3, the solution is hypertonic; water flows out of the cell, causing it to shrink and shrivel (crenation). Note that tonicity is always a comparison — the same solution can be hypotonic to one cell type and hypertonic to another, depending on that cell's internal solute concentration.

Problem-solving application. Tonicity explains many real-world biological and medical decisions. Intravenous (IV) fluids given in hospitals are formulated to be isotonic to blood cells (commonly 0.9% saline or 5% dextrose) precisely to avoid damaging red blood cells — an error here could cause cells to burst or shrivel, with serious clinical consequences. Similarly, when preserving food with salt or sugar, the goal is to create a strongly hypertonic environment: this draws water out of bacterial and fungal cells, dehydrating and killing them, which is why salted or sugared foods resist spoilage. In plants, hypotonic conditions are actually beneficial in moderation, since water entering plant cells builds internal pressure (turgor pressure) against the rigid cell wall, keeping the plant upright — a wilted plant is often one sitting in soil that has become hypertonic relative to its root cells, as can happen with over-fertilization. In each case, correctly identifying the direction of osmotic water flow — by comparing solute concentrations — is the key problem-solving step.
