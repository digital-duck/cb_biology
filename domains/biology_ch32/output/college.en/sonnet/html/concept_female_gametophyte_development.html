Female Gametophyte Development

Inside the ovule, a diploid megaspore mother cell (megasporocyte) undergoes meiosis in a process called megasporogenesis, producing four haploid megaspores. In most flowering plants, three of these degenerate, leaving a single functional megaspore. This survivor does not become a gamete directly; instead it develops into the female gametophyte—the multicellular, haploid structure that will house the egg—through a second process called megagametogenesis.

The functional megaspore enlarges and undergoes three rounds of free nuclear mitosis without immediate cell wall formation, producing eight haploid nuclei within one large cell. These nuclei then migrate to characteristic positions and cellularization occurs, partitioning the cytoplasm into seven cells containing eight nuclei total—this is the mature embryo sac. At the micropylar end (nearest the point of pollen tube entry) sit three cells: the egg cell, flanked by two synergids that help guide the pollen tube and facilitate sperm delivery. At the opposite, chalazal end lie three antipodal cells, whose function is less critical and which often degenerate before fertilization. The center holds one large central cell containing two polar nuclei, which remain in a shared cytoplasm rather than separating into distinct cells.

Consider a botanist examining an ovule under a microscope who counts only seven cells but must confirm eight nuclei before classifying the embryo sac as mature. Locating the central cell and finding two nuclei there—rather than one—confirms the polar nuclei are present and the gametophyte is developmentally complete, distinguishing it from an immature four-nucleate stage.

This 7-cell/8-nucleate architecture directly enables double fertilization, a hallmark of flowering plants: one sperm nucleus fuses with the egg cell to form the diploid zygote, while the second sperm nucleus fuses with both polar nuclei to form a triploid ($3n$) primary endosperm nucleus, which develops into nutritive endosperm tissue. Applying this concept, a plant breeder analyzing seed abortion in a hybrid cross can localize the defect: if embryos form but endosperm fails, the problem likely lies in polar nuclei fusion or subsequent triploid tissue development, not in egg cell fertilization itself. Understanding which embryo-sac component corresponds to which post-fertilization structure allows precise diagnosis of where reproductive failure occurs.
