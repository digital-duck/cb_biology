Protist Pathogens

Protists are typically thought of as harmless single-celled organisms drifting in pond water, but several lineages have evolved into potent pathogens that have shaped human history as much as any bacterium or virus. Two genera illustrate this best: Plasmodium, the cause of malaria, and Trypanosoma, responsible for African sleeping sickness and Chagas disease. Both are transmitted by insect vectors, both have complex multi-host life cycles, and both remain leading causes of illness and death in tropical regions today.

Plasmodium is spread by female Anopheles mosquitoes. When a mosquito bites, it injects sporozoites into the bloodstream; these travel to the liver, multiply, and then rupture into the blood to invade red blood cells. Inside red blood cells the parasite replicates and eventually bursts them synchronously, releasing toxins and triggering the fever cycles characteristic of malaria. This synchronized bursting is why malaria fevers often recur every 48 or 72 hours, depending on the Plasmodium species involved. Trypanosoma brucei (sleeping sickness) is transmitted by the tsetse fly and lives extracellularly in blood and eventually the central nervous system, while Trypanosoma cruzi (Chagas disease) enters through the bite wound of a triatomine "kissing bug" and invades heart and gut tissue.

A key problem-solving skill in parasitology is reasoning about why these infections are so hard to treat and vaccinate against. Trypanosoma evades the immune system through antigenic variation: it carries hundreds of genes for a surface coat protein and switches which one it expresses, so that just as the immune system builds antibodies against one variant, the parasite population shifts to a new one. This is a direct analogy to why influenza requires a new vaccine each year, only far more extreme — a useful comparison for students working through immune-evasion problems.

Public health interventions follow logically from life-cycle biology: vector control (bed nets, insecticide) interrupts transmission for Plasmodium, while housing improvements reduce triatomine bug contact for Chagas disease. Understanding each stage of the life cycle — vector, liver, bloodstream, or intracellular niche — reveals exactly where a drug, vaccine, or intervention could break the cycle, which is the central strategic question in combating any vector-borne parasitic disease.
