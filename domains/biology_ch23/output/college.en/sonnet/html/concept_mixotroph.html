Mixotroph

A mixotroph is an organism that switches between, or simultaneously uses, photoautotrophy (making food via photosynthesis) and heterotrophy (consuming organic matter from other organisms) to meet its energy and nutrient needs. Rather than being locked into one nutritional mode, a mixotroph adjusts its strategy based on environmental conditions such as light availability, prey density, or nutrient scarcity. This flexibility distinguishes mixotrophs from strict autotrophs (like most land plants) and strict heterotrophs (like animals), and it is especially common among protists and some bacteria.

Worked example. Consider Dinobryon, a freshwater flagellate algae. In sunlit, nutrient-rich water, it photosynthesizes like a typical alga, using chlorophyll to convert light energy into sugars. But when dissolved phosphorus or nitrogen becomes scarce — nutrients photosynthesis alone cannot supply — Dinobryon switches strategy: it engulfs bacteria as prey, absorbing their organic nitrogen and phosphorus directly. The same individual organism is therefore autotrophic on one day and partly heterotrophic the next, depending purely on what the environment can and cannot provide. This is not two organisms cooperating (as in a lichen symbiosis) but one cell running two distinct metabolic programs.

Problem-solving application. Mixotrophy explains ecological patterns that a simple "producer vs. consumer" model cannot. Suppose you are studying a lake and observe that a mixotrophic algal population blooms even in late summer, when light is strong but dissolved phosphate has dropped to near zero — a condition where purely photosynthetic algae typically decline. A student modeling the food web with a rigid trophic-level diagram (producers → primary consumers → secondary consumers) would incorrectly place this organism in a single box and fail to predict its persistence. The correct approach is to recognize the organism as functionally two things at once: track its carbon budget as coming from light when nutrients are sufficient, and from ingested bacterial prey when nutrients are limiting. This dual-pathway thinking is essential in real ecosystem modeling — for instance, in explaining why certain harmful algal blooms persist under nutrient stress, or why some coral symbionts survive bleaching events by supplementing photosynthesis with particle feeding. Recognizing mixotrophy shifts the modeling task from classifying an organism into one category to tracking which nutritional pathway dominates under which specific, measurable environmental condition.
