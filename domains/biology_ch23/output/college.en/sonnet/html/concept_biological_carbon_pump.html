Biological Carbon Pump

The biological carbon pump is the sequence of processes by which carbon dioxide dissolved in ocean surface waters is converted into organic matter by photosynthetic protists — phytoplankton such as diatoms and coccolithophores — and then transported to the deep ocean and seafloor as that organic matter sinks. Unlike the physical (or "solubility") pump, which moves CO₂ downward through ocean circulation and temperature-driven gas solubility, the biological pump depends on life: photosynthesis fixes carbon into cell tissue and shells, and gravity does the rest once the organisms die or are consumed and excreted as dense fecal pellets.

Worked example. Consider a patch of surface ocean where phytoplankton fix carbon at a rate of 50 grams of carbon per square meter per year (a typical value for productive coastal waters). Not all of this carbon reaches the deep ocean — most is recycled by bacteria and zooplankton in the sunlit surface layer before it can sink. Oceanographers use the export ratio (or e-ratio), the fraction of fixed carbon that escapes the surface layer and sinks below roughly 100 meters. A typical e-ratio is 0.1–0.2. If the export ratio here is 0.15, then the carbon exported downward is $50 \times 0.15 = 7.5$ grams of carbon per square meter per year. Only a further fraction of that survives remineralization (bacterial decomposition) on the way down to reach the seafloor and be buried — often less than 1% of the original surface production. This step-by-step attenuation explains why the pump, despite processing enormous quantities of carbon, buries only a small residual fraction long-term.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a research team measures primary production of 80 g C/m²/yr in an ocean region and estimates that 10 g C/m²/yr is exported past 100 m depth. What is the export ratio, and if remineralization removes 90% of exported carbon before it reaches the seafloor at 4,000 m, how much carbon is ultimately buried? The export ratio is $10/80 = 0.125$, or 12.5%. Of the 10 g exported, 90% is remineralized, leaving $10 \times 0.10 = 1$ g C/m²/yr reaching the seafloor. This type of calculation is exactly how scientists estimate global ocean carbon burial rates and assess how sensitive the biological pump is to changes in phytoplankton productivity, ocean warming, or acidification — all of which can alter export and remineralization efficiency and thus the ocean's long-term capacity to sequester atmospheric CO₂.
