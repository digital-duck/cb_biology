Concept Book: Carbon Dating

Biology Ch02 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Carbon dating is the culmination of everything developed in this book: exponential decay, half-life, and the mathematics of first-order rate processes converge here into a tool that lets scientists read the age of once-living matter directly from a physical measurement. The technique rests on a single quantitative law. Living organisms continuously exchange carbon with the atmosphere, maintaining a fixed ratio of radioactive $^{14}\text{C}$ to stable $^{12}\text{C}$. At death, exchange stops, and $^{14}\text{C}$ decays according to

$$N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}, \qquad t_{1/2} \approx 5730 \text{ years}.$$

Measuring the present activity $N(t)$ against the known initial activity $N_0$ and solving for $t$ turns a decay curve into a calendar. This is the natural endpoint of the subject because it demonstrates that an abstract exponential model, once calibrated against a physical constant, becomes a precise instrument for measuring elapsed time — the very thing calculus and rate equations were built to describe.

The reach of this idea extends across every domain touched by this book. In archaeology, it dates organic artifacts, textiles, and skeletal remains, anchoring human history to numerical timelines. In geology and paleoclimatology, it constrains the age of sediment layers, ice cores, and fossilized organisms. In art authentication, it distinguishes genuine antiquities from modern forgeries. In forensic science, it estimates time since death in decomposed remains. Each application is simply the same governing equation applied to a different sample and a different question, underscoring how one mathematical relationship can answer age questions across biology, earth science, and the humanities.

As a next step, consider working through a full archaeological dating problem: given a measured $^{14}\text{C}$ activity of 62.5% of the modern standard in a wooden artifact, use SymPy to solve for the artifact's age and discuss the uncertainty introduced by measurement error in $N(t)/N_0$.

import sympy as sp

t, lam = sp.symbols('t lambda', positive=True)
half_life = 5730
lam_val = sp.ln(2) / half_life

ratio = sp.Rational(625, 1000)  # N(t)/N0 = 0.625
age = sp.solve(sp.Eq(ratio, sp.exp(-lam_val * t)), t)[0]
sp.pprint(sp.nsimplify(age))
print(sp.N(age, 6), "years")
