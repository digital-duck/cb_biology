Every concept in this book — spores, alternation of generations, rhizoids, peat accumulation, vascular tissue in its earliest form — converges on a single question: what do seedless plants actually do for the planet and for people? This is the natural endpoint because biology is not just classification; it is consequence. Understanding a moss's life cycle is only half the story until you can explain why a bog full of Sphagnum locks away more carbon than most forests, or why a fern-lined slope resists erosion better than bare soil. Ecological and human uses close the loop between structure and impact.

Consider peatlands, built almost entirely from partially decayed Sphagnum moss. They cover about 3% of Earth's land surface yet store roughly twice as much carbon as all the world's forests combined, because the moss's acidic, low-oxygen environment slows decomposition to a crawl. A logging company deciding whether to drain a peat bog for timber access, or a climate policy analyst estimating national carbon budgets, is directly applying what you now know about seedless plant biology — the spongy leaf cells that hold water, the lack of true roots that keeps the moss shallow and undemanding, the reproductive strategy that lets it spread across disturbed ground quickly.

That same logic extends outward. In agriculture and horticulture, sphagnum peat moss is a workhorse soil amendment because its cell structure retains water while resisting compaction — a direct application of the anatomy covered earlier in this book. In environmental monitoring, liverworts and mosses serve as bioindicators of air and water quality: their lack of a cuticle and direct absorption of nutrients and pollutants through their surfaces make them exquisitely sensitive to acid rain and heavy metals, turning a structural "limitation" into a diagnostic tool. In restoration ecology, ferns and mosses are often the first colonizers deployed on degraded slopes, dunes, and post-fire landscapes, because their spore-based reproduction allows rapid, wind-dispersed recolonization without the developmental lag seed plants require.
