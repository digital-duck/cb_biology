Lignin

Cellulose gives plant cell walls tensile strength, but strength alone doesn't explain how a tree trunk stays rigid under its own weight or how water travels upward through narrow vessels without leaking out. Lignin is the answer to both problems. It is a complex, highly cross-linked polymer built from phenolic subunits (monolignols) that fills the spaces between cellulose microfibrils in the cell wall, especially in vascular tissue like xylem. Unlike cellulose, which is a regular chain of glucose units, lignin has an irregular, three-dimensional network structure — more like a hardened resin than a fiber. This structure makes it rigid and, critically, hydrophobic: lignin repels water rather than absorbing it.

Consider what happens without lignin. Water moving through a plant's xylem vessels is under negative pressure (tension), pulled upward by transpiration at the leaves. If the vessel walls were made only of cellulose, which readily absorbs water and swells, the vessels would collapse inward under this tension, like a soft straw collapsing when you suck too hard. Lignin's waterproofing rigidity prevents this collapse, keeping the plumbing intact even in tall trees moving water dozens of meters against gravity. This is why lignin is concentrated precisely in tissues bearing mechanical load or transporting water — xylem, bark, and woody stems — rather than uniformly throughout the plant.

Lignin's evolutionary and structural role becomes clear in a problem-solving context: why can plants grow tall, while algae in water cannot exceed a few meters even with unlimited nutrients? Algae are buoyed by water and don't need self-supporting rigidity, so many never evolved lignin. Land plants faced a different constraint — gravity, with no water to counteract it — and lignified tissue solved that engineering problem, enabling the evolution of trees over 100 meters tall.

This trade-off has a cost: lignin is metabolically expensive to produce and very difficult to break down, since few organisms have enzymes that cleave its irregular bonds. That is precisely why wood decomposes slowly and why lignin removal (delignification) is a major industrial challenge in papermaking and biofuel production — a direct, practical consequence of the same rigidity that makes it useful to the plant.
