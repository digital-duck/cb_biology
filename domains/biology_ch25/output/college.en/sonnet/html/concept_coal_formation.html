Coal Formation

Coal is a sedimentary rock formed from the compressed, chemically altered remains of ancient plants. Most of the coal burned today originated during the Carboniferous Period (roughly 359–299 million years ago), when vast swamp forests dominated by seedless vascular plants—giant lycopods, horsetails, and ferns—covered low-lying, waterlogged landscapes. When these plants died, they fell into oxygen-poor swamp water rather than decomposing fully in open air. Anaerobic conditions slowed the microbial breakdown that normally recycles plant material into the atmosphere, allowing partially decayed organic matter to accumulate as thick layers of peat.

Over millions of years, peat became buried under sediment—sand, mud, and later rock. Heat and pressure from this overlying burden progressively drove off water and volatile compounds, concentrating carbon. This process, called coalification, proceeds through recognizable stages: peat becomes lignite (soft, brownish, low carbon content), then sub-bituminous and bituminous coal (harder, higher carbon and energy content), and under the most extreme heat and pressure, anthracite (dense, nearly pure carbon, highest energy yield per unit mass). Each stage represents deeper burial and longer geologic time.

Coal's usefulness as fuel traces directly to this chemistry. Photosynthesis in Carboniferous plants captured solar energy and stored it in carbon-hydrogen bonds; coalification concentrated that stored energy by removing oxygen, hydrogen, and water while carbon content rose. Burning coal breaks those bonds, releasing the ancient solar energy as heat—which is why coal is called a fossil fuel and why it is non-renewable on human timescales: the swamp conditions and geologic burial required to form it took millions of years and cannot be recreated on demand.

This has direct problem-solving relevance in energy and environmental science. Because coal rank (lignite through anthracite) correlates with carbon content and energy density, engineers select coal type based on required heat output and combustion efficiency for a given power plant. Estimating a region's coal reserves involves reconstructing which rock layers correspond to Carboniferous-age swamp deposits, since coal only forms within specific geologic sequences. Understanding coalification also explains why burning coal releases carbon that photosynthesis removed from the atmosphere over 300 million years ago, informing debates about carbon budgets: combustion returns geologically sequestered carbon to the atmosphere far faster than it was originally stored, a key quantitative point in climate policy analysis.
