Seedless Vascular Plants

Seedless vascular plants — ferns, horsetails, and lycophytes (club mosses) — represent the first plant lineage to evolve true vascular tissue: xylem for transporting water and dissolved minerals upward, and phloem for distributing sugars produced by photosynthesis. This internal plumbing let these plants grow taller and thicker than the earlier bryophytes (mosses, liverworts), since a vascular system provides both structural support and a way to move resources over longer distances. Vascular tissue also enabled true roots (for anchorage and absorption) and true leaves (for expanded photosynthetic surface area), features bryophytes lack.

Despite this engineering advance, seedless vascular plants retain one major constraint from their bryophyte ancestors: reproduction still depends on liquid water. Their life cycle alternates between a dominant, independent sporophyte (the familiar leafy fern you see growing) and a small, short-lived gametophyte. The sporophyte produces haploid spores by meiosis; these spores germinate into gametophytes that produce flagellated sperm and stationary eggs. The sperm must swim through a film of water to reach and fertilize the egg — exactly as in mosses — which is why ferns thrive in moist, shaded habitats and are absent from deserts.

Worked example. Consider a fern population in a forest that experiences an unusually dry spring. Spore production and dispersal (a wind-driven, water-independent process) proceed normally, and gametophytes germinate on damp soil. But fertilization fails across much of the population because there isn't enough surface water for sperm to swim to eggs. The observable consequence is a "reproductive bottleneck" that year — abundant gametophytes, but few new sporophytes recruited into the population — even though the adult ferns themselves survive the drought using their vascular tissue and root systems.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you are a field ecologist tasked with explaining why a fern species is restricted to a narrow streamside habitat while a nearby flowering plant occupies the whole hillside. Applying the concept, you'd reason: the flowering plant's pollen and seeds don't require external water for fertilization or dispersal, whereas the fern's flagellated sperm do. Predict, then, that any climate trend toward reduced spring moisture would shrink the fern's viable habitat faster than the flowering plant's — a testable hypothesis linking reproductive biology directly to geographic distribution.
