Mycorrhizae

Mycorrhizae are mutualistic partnerships between fungi and plant roots. Fine fungal filaments called hyphae either penetrate root cortical cells (arbuscular mycorrhizae, the most common type) or wrap around root surfaces without entering cells (ectomycorrhizae, typical of forest trees like pines and oaks). In both cases, the fungal network vastly extends the effective surface area of the root system, reaching pockets of soil moisture and mineral nutrients — especially phosphorus and nitrogen — that root hairs alone cannot access. In exchange, the plant supplies the fungus with sugars produced through photosynthesis, since fungi cannot photosynthesize and depend entirely on organic carbon from a host or from decomposing matter.

Worked example. Consider a pine seedling growing in phosphorus-poor soil. Without a fungal partner, its roots can only absorb phosphate ions that diffuse within a few millimeters of the root surface, and phosphate diffuses slowly because it binds tightly to soil particles. An ectomycorrhizal fungus colonizing the root system extends thread-like hyphae up to several centimeters beyond the depletion zone. Because hyphae are only a few micrometers wide, the fungus can explore a soil volume that is orders of magnitude larger than the root itself for a much lower carbon cost per unit of soil explored than growing equivalent root tissue. The seedling essentially "outsources" nutrient foraging to a partner that is more efficient at it, while paying in sugar rather than in root biomass.

Problem-solving application. Suppose an agricultural researcher is designing a low-phosphorus-fertilizer strategy for a crop rotation. Knowing that mycorrhizal associations reduce a plant's dependence on synthetic phosphate, the researcher must decide which practices to avoid: excessive tillage physically shreds fungal networks, and very high fertilizer application suppresses the symbiosis because the plant has less incentive to "pay" for fungal partners when nutrients are already abundant in the soil solution. A sound recommendation would be to reduce tillage, apply phosphorus fertilizer only at levels that do not fully saturate soil nutrient demand, and choose crop species known to form strong mycorrhizal associations (such as many legumes and cereals) rather than species in families that resist colonization (such as brassicas, including cabbage and canola). This reasoning illustrates the general problem-solving pattern for mycorrhizae: identify the resource each partner needs, then ask which management choices preserve versus disrupt the exchange.
