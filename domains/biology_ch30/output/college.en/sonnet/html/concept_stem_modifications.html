Stem Modifications

Not all stems stand upright above ground bearing leaves. Evolution has repurposed the basic stem plan — a structure with nodes, internodes, and axillary buds — into forms specialized for storage, propagation, protection, and climbing. What distinguishes a modified stem from a root, even when it grows underground, is the presence of nodes and buds; roots never have these.

Underground storage and spread. A rhizome (ginger, iris) is a horizontal stem that grows just below the soil surface, storing carbohydrates and sending up new shoots at nodes, allowing the plant to spread clonally. A corm (gladiolus, crocus) is a short, vertical, solid stem surrounded by protective papery scale leaves — often confused with a bulb, but a bulb (onion, tulip) is mostly fleshy leaf tissue wrapped around a tiny central stem, the leaves themselves storing the food. A tuber (potato) is a swollen underground stem tip packed with starch, its "eyes" being nodes with dormant buds — which is why a single potato can be cut into pieces and each piece, if it contains an eye, can grow into a new plant.

Aboveground spread and defense. A stolon or runner (strawberry) is a horizontal stem that grows along the soil surface, rooting at nodes to establish genetically identical daughter plants. A tendril (grape, pea) is a slender, coiling stem modified for climbing, wrapping around supports to lift the plant toward light. A thorn (citrus, hawthorn) is a hardened, pointed stem modified for defense against herbivores — distinct from a prickle (rose), which is an outgrowth of the epidermis, not a stem at all.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you are given an unlabeled plant sample with a swollen underground structure and asked to classify it. The diagnostic question is always: does it have nodes and buds, or scale leaves? If the structure is solid stem tissue with a papery covering and buds only at the top, it is a corm. If it is fleshy leaf layers surrounding a small stem, it is a bulb. If it has small depressions ("eyes") scattered over its surface, each with a bud, it is a tuber. This same logic explains a practical agricultural fact: potatoes are propagated vegetatively by planting tuber pieces, while onions are propagated from bulbs or seeds — because only the tuber's eyes carry the meristematic tissue needed to generate a whole new plant.
