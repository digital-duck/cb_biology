Root Anatomy

A root's internal structure is organized in concentric layers, each with a distinct role in absorbing water, transporting nutrients, and controlling what enters the plant's vascular system. Moving from the outside in, a cross-section of a young dicot root reveals five key tissue regions: the epidermis, the cortex, the endodermis, the pericycle, and the vascular stele.

The epidermis is the outermost single layer of cells, specialized for absorption. Many epidermal cells extend into root hairs, which dramatically increase surface area for water and mineral uptake. Beneath it lies the cortex, a thick region of loosely packed parenchyma cells that stores starch and allows water to move inward through the spaces between cells. This unregulated pathway through cell walls and intercellular spaces is called the apoplast route.

The endodermis is a single layer of tightly packed cells that marks the innermost boundary of the cortex. Its defining feature is the Casparian strip, a band of waxy, waterproof suberin embedded in the radial and transverse walls of each endodermal cell. Because water cannot pass through suberin, the Casparian strip blocks the apoplast pathway at this layer, forcing all water and dissolved minerals to cross the plasma membrane of an endodermal cell to continue inward. This is the plant's checkpoint: membrane transport proteins selectively admit or exclude specific ions, giving the plant control over its internal chemistry rather than passively absorbing whatever is in the soil water.

Just inside the endodermis is the pericycle, a layer of cells that retains the ability to divide. This is significant beyond structure — the pericycle is the site where lateral (branch) roots originate, growing outward through the cortex and epidermis.

At the center is the vascular stele, containing xylem (typically arranged in a star-like pattern in dicots) and phloem, which transport water/minerals upward and sugars throughout the plant, respectively.

Applying the concept: If a root's endodermis is experimentally damaged so the Casparian strip no longer forms, predict the effect on the plant's ability to exclude toxic ions like excess sodium from the soil solution. Since the apoplast route would now bypass the membrane checkpoint, ions could flow unregulated into the vascular stele, undermining the plant's selective uptake and increasing vulnerability to salt stress — illustrating why this single band of waxy tissue is disproportionately important to whole-plant physiology.
