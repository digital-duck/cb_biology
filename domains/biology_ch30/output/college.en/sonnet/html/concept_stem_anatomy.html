Stem Anatomy

A stem is not a uniform rod of tissue but an organized system built from three tissue types arranged in a predictable pattern. The dermal tissue forms the outer protective layer (epidermis, often covered by a waxy cuticle). The ground tissue fills most of the interior, providing storage, photosynthesis (in green stems), and structural support. The vascular tissue — xylem and phloem — runs through the stem in discrete strands called vascular bundles, transporting water/minerals upward and sugars in both directions. Along the stem's length, nodes are the points where leaves, buds, or branches attach, and internodes are the segments of stem between successive nodes.

Consider a young sunflower stem in cross-section. Cutting it and viewing it under a microscope, you'd see the epidermis as a thin outer rim, the ground tissue (mostly parenchyma, called pith in the center and cortex near the edge) making up the bulk of the tissue, and a ring of vascular bundles positioned near the boundary between cortex and pith — each bundle containing xylem toward the inside and phloem toward the outside. This arrangement is characteristic of eudicots; in monocots like corn, the vascular bundles are instead scattered throughout the ground tissue rather than forming a ring. Recognizing this difference from a single cross-section is often enough to classify a plant as monocot or eudicot without ever seeing its flower.

This anatomy also explains a mechanical puzzle: why does cutting a stem at a node (versus mid-internode) produce different results for propagation? Nodes contain axillary buds with actively dividing meristematic tissue and a denser convergence of vascular strands feeding those buds, while internodes are comparatively vascular "highway" segments without growth centers. This is why gardeners take stem cuttings that include at least one node — an internode-only cutting has transport tissue but no meristem to regenerate roots or shoots from. A related applied question: if a horticulturist wants to estimate how many new shoots a cutting could produce, they can literally count the nodes along the stem segment, since each node is a potential branch point. Understanding stem anatomy therefore converts a visual/structural feature into a predictive, countable variable for practical decisions in agriculture, propagation, and plant diagnostics.
