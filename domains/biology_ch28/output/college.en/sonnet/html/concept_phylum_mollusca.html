Phylum Mollusca

Mollusca is the second-largest animal phylum, encompassing over 85,000 living species built on a shared body plan: a muscular foot for locomotion or prey capture, a visceral mass containing internal organs, and a mantle—a fold of tissue that secretes a calcium carbonate shell and encloses a water-filled mantle cavity used for respiration. Many mollusks also possess a radula, a chitinous, tooth-covered ribbon used for scraping or cutting food. Despite being unsegmented protostomes (their embryonic blastopore becomes the mouth), mollusks display extraordinary diversity, organized into classes such as Gastropoda (snails, slugs), Bivalvia (clams, oysters), and Cephalopoda (octopuses, squid).

Worked example. Consider the taxonomic puzzle of classifying an unfamiliar marine animal. A field biologist finds a soft-bodied creature with two hinged shells, no distinct head, and gills used for both breathing and filter-feeding. Applying the diagnostic traits of Mollusca: the presence of a mantle (secreting the two shells), a reduced foot for burrowing, and no radula (since bivalves feed by filtering rather than scraping) correctly places this organism in class Bivalvia. Contrast this with an animal having a single coiled shell, a distinct head with tentacles, and a radula for grazing algae—these traits identify a gastropod instead. The presence or absence of specific structures (shell number, radula, foot shape) functions as a systematic key for placing organisms within molluscan classes.

Problem-solving application. Evolutionary biologists use molluscan structures to infer functional trade-offs. Suppose you are asked to predict which class would be most vulnerable to ocean acidification and why. Since acidification lowers seawater pH and reduces carbonate ion availability, organisms that invest heavily in calcium carbonate shells—like bivalves and many gastropods—face impaired shell formation, while cephalopods, which have internalized or lost their shells (as in octopuses), are comparatively buffered. This reasoning illustrates a broader problem-solving skill: linking anatomical structure (shell composition, dependence on the mantle) to ecological vulnerability. Similarly, understanding the radula's role explains why some gastropods (like cone snails) have modified it into a venomous harpoon, demonstrating how a single conserved structure can diversify into radically different survival strategies across a phylum.
