Phylum Annelida

Annelids are bilaterally symmetrical protostomes defined by true metameric segmentation: the body is divided into a linear series of repeating units, or segments, each containing its own set of nerves, blood vessels, muscles, and excretory organs (nephridia). This segmentation is not merely superficial rings on the outside — internal organs are compartmentalized by tissue partitions called septa, so each segment functions as a semi-independent module. The phylum includes earthworms (Oligochaeta), marine bristle worms (Polychaeta), and leeches (Hirudinea), all sharing a coelom (fluid-filled body cavity) that acts as a hydrostatic skeleton.

Worked example. Consider why segmentation is adaptive for burrowing locomotion. An earthworm moves by alternating waves of circular and longitudinal muscle contraction along its body. Because each segment has its own septum-enclosed coelomic fluid, a contraction in one segment doesn't dissipate pressure across the whole body — it stays localized, creating a wave of elongation and thickening that pushes against burrow walls. Bristles (setae) anchor segments during each phase so the worm doesn't slip backward. Segmentation, in effect, converts one long hydraulic system into many independently controllable pistons, giving the worm fine-grained control over which part of its body pushes, anchors, or elongates at a given moment.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a researcher observes an earthworm and a roundworm (phylum Nematoda, unsegmented) moving through soil, and wants to explain why the earthworm can burrow through denser soil more efficiently. The reasoning follows directly from structure: the roundworm's unsegmented, unpartitioned pseudocoelom transmits pressure changes throughout its entire body simultaneously, limiting it to a whip-like thrashing motion. The earthworm's segmented, septate coelom allows localized peristaltic waves, concentrating force at the burrowing tip while the rest of the body remains anchored. This same reasoning generalizes: whenever you see repeated internal partitioning in a biological structure, ask what independent control or redundancy it buys the organism — in annelids, it buys precise, regionalized locomotion and, evolutionarily, a body plan flexible enough to specialize segments for feeding, reproduction, or sensory function, as seen in the differentiated head segments of polychaetes.
