Molluscan Body Plan

Mollusks (phylum Mollusca) are astonishingly diverse — snails, clams, octopuses, and chitons look almost nothing alike — yet all of them are built from the same four structural modules. The foot is a muscular organ used for locomotion, burrowing, or (in cephalopods) modified into arms and tentacles. The visceral mass sits above the foot and houses the digestive, excretory, and reproductive organs. The mantle is a sheet of tissue that wraps around the visceral mass, secretes the calcium-carbonate shell, and encloses a mantle cavity used for respiration. The radula is a rasping, tongue-like ribbon of chitinous teeth used to scrape or cut food. Understanding this shared plan lets you predict how a given lineage has modified each module to fit its lifestyle.

Consider a garden snail (a gastropod) versus a squid (a cephalopod). Both have a mantle secreting a shell — visible and coiled in the snail, reduced to an internal pen in the squid. Both have a radula for feeding — the snail's scrapes algae off surfaces, the squid's is paired with a sharp beak to tear prey. The key difference is the foot: in the snail it is a flat, ventral gliding surface, while in the squid it has been evolutionarily repurposed into a funnel (siphon) and arms, enabling jet propulsion instead of crawling. The visceral mass in both cases remains tucked beneath the mantle, protected and centralized.

This body-plan framework is a diagnostic tool: given an unfamiliar mollusk, you can identify its class by asking how each of the four structures has been modified. Suppose you're handed a bivalve, like a clam. Applying the framework: the foot is hatchet-shaped for burrowing into sediment; the mantle is split into two lobes that secrete a two-part hinged shell; the visceral mass is reduced and simplified since bivalves are filter feeders; and — the diagnostic surprise — the radula is entirely absent, because filter-feeding via gills replaces the need to scrape food. Recognizing that a "typical" structure has been lost, rather than assuming an error, is exactly the kind of inference comparative anatomy trains you to make: identify the shared plan first, then interpret each deviation as an adaptation to a specific ecological niche.
