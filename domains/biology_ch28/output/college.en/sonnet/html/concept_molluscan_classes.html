Molluscan Classes

Mollusca is the second-largest animal phylum, and its diversity is organized around three body components that evolution has repeatedly reshaped: the muscular foot, the calcareous shell, and the mantle, a tissue layer that secretes the shell and encloses a water-filled mantle cavity. The eight recognized classes are distinguished largely by how these three structures are modified. Gastropoda (snails, slugs) have a broad creeping foot and typically one coiled shell; Bivalvia (clams, mussels) have a hatchet-shaped foot for burrowing and a shell divided into two hinged valves; Cephalopoda (squid, octopus) have the foot modified into arms/tentacles and a funnel for jet propulsion, with the shell reduced or internal; Polyplacophora (chitons) have a flattened foot and a shell of eight overlapping plates; Scaphopoda (tusk shells) have a tubular shell open at both ends; Monoplacophora have a single cap-like shell and serially repeated organs; and Aplacophora and Caudofoveata (sometimes grouped as Aculifera) lack a shell entirely, retaining only calcareous spicules in the mantle.

Worked example. Suppose you find a marine animal with a single external shell, a broad flat foot, and a radula (rasping "tongue") it uses to scrape algae off rocks. Which class is it? The single coiled or cap-shaped shell plus creeping foot points to Gastropoda, not Bivalvia (which has two valves and no radula) or Polyplacophora (which has eight plates, not one shell).

Applying the framework. Classification here is a diagnostic tool: when confronted with an unfamiliar mollusk, you systematically ask three questions. (1) Shell state — absent, one piece, two valves, eight plates, or a single tube? (2) Foot modification — creeping, burrowing, tentacled, or reduced? (3) Habitat/locomotion clue — sessile filter-feeder, active predator, or grazer? Working through these in order rapidly narrows the eight-class field to one answer, the same logic taxonomists use in dichotomous keys. This structural approach also explains major functional trade-offs: cephalopods traded shell protection for speed and intelligence, while bivalves traded mobility for a fully enclosing, predator-resistant shell — showing how a single ancestral body plan diversified into strikingly different ecological strategies.
