Molecular Phylogenetic Revision

For most of the twentieth century, biologists classified animals by comparing anatomy: skeletal structure, body plan, number of limbs, presence of a notochord or shell. This approach, called morphological classification, produced a tree of life that seemed reasonable but rested on an unreliable assumption — that similar-looking structures always indicate shared ancestry. Since the 1990s, the ability to sequence DNA and compare it across species has overturned many of these groupings. Molecular phylogenetics compares the actual genetic sequences of organisms, and because DNA mutates at a roughly predictable rate, the number and pattern of differences between two species' genomes serve as a molecular clock — a quantitative measure of how long ago two lineages diverged from a common ancestor.

Consider whales and hippopotamuses. Morphologically, whales look nothing like any land mammal — no hind legs, a fluke instead of a tail, blowholes instead of nostrils. Classical taxonomy placed whales in their own separate order, distant from hoofed mammals. But molecular data — particularly shared, rare genetic mutations in a family of genes and retrotransposon insertions found in both whale and hippo genomes but absent in other mammals — showed that whales are more closely related to hippos than hippos are to pigs or cows. This forced a reclassification: whales are now placed inside the order Artiodactyla (even-toed ungulates), a group defined by molecular kinship rather than by having legs at all.

The same logic drives problem-solving in the field today. When biologists want to test a proposed relationship between two species, they sequence a shared gene — often mitochondrial DNA or ribosomal RNA, which mutate slowly and are present in nearly all animals — from both organisms and from several outgroup species. They align the sequences and count the number of base-pair differences at each position. Fewer differences suggest a more recent common ancestor; more differences suggest a deeper split. Software tools then build a tree (a cladogram) that minimizes the total number of evolutionary changes needed to explain the observed data, a principle called maximum parsimony. A student applying this method to real GenBank sequence data must decide which gene to use, how to align sequences correcting for insertions and deletions, and how to interpret a resulting tree that may still be reshaped by the next dataset — reclassification is an ongoing, evidence-driven process, not a finished catalog.
