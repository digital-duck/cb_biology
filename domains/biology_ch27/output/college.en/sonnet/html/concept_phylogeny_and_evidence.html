Phylogeny And Evidence

A phylogeny is a hypothesis about the evolutionary relationships among a set of organisms, represented as a branching tree diagram. Each branch point, or node, represents a common ancestor from which two or more descendant lineages diverged. The tips of the tree represent species (living or extinct) for which we have data. Building an accurate phylogeny is fundamentally a problem of inference: we cannot observe the ancestral past directly, so we reconstruct it from evidence left in three main forms — morphology (shared physical structures), the fossil record (direct physical remains with a time stamp), and molecular data (similarities and differences in DNA or protein sequences).

The central logic scientists use is shared derived characteristics (synapomorphies): traits that arose in a common ancestor and were inherited by all its descendants, distinguishing them from other lineages. Shared ancestral traits present in many distantly related groups are less useful for grouping, because they don't indicate recent common ancestry.

Worked example. Suppose you're classifying four vertebrates — a lizard, a crocodile, a bird, and a mammal — using the trait "presence of a four-chambered heart." Birds and mammals have this trait; lizards and crocodiles do not (crocodiles have a nearly four-chambered heart, but not fully divided). If you built a tree using only this trait, you'd group birds with mammals. But molecular and fossil evidence (skull structure, egg types) instead places birds as most closely related to crocodiles, within a group called Archosauria, with mammals branching off much earlier. This reveals that the four-chambered heart evolved independently in birds and mammals — a case of convergent evolution, not shared ancestry. This is why phylogenies today rely on combining many independent lines of evidence rather than a single trait.

Application. When constructing or evaluating a phylogeny, apply the principle of parsimony: among competing trees that explain the data, prefer the one requiring the fewest independent evolutionary changes (fewest convergences or reversals). Given a data matrix of species and traits, you can count the number of trait changes required by each candidate tree topology and select the tree with the minimum total. Molecular data adds statistical power here because DNA sequences provide thousands of independent characters (nucleotide sites), making convergence at every site improbable and allowing more confident, testable phylogenetic hypotheses.
