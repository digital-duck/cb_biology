Genome Fusion

Genome fusion refers to the merging of two distinct prokaryotic genomes into a single evolutionary lineage, most plausibly through an act of endosymbiosis: one prokaryotic cell engulfing or stably incorporating another, such that the two genomes come to coexist and eventually cooperate within one organism. This idea is central to explaining a puzzling feature of eukaryotic cells — they carry genes that trace to two very different prokaryotic ancestries, one archaeal and one bacterial, layered together rather than descending from a single ancestral genome.

The clearest worked example is the origin of the mitochondrion. Comparative genomics shows that mitochondrial DNA closely resembles that of alphaproteobacteria, while the genes governing the eukaryotic host cell's core information-processing machinery (DNA replication, transcription, translation) resemble archaeal genes, specifically those of a lineage now placed within the Asgard archaea. Under the fusion model, an archaeal host cell took up an alphaproteobacterial symbiont. Over time, most of the bacterium's genes were either lost or transferred into the host's own chromosome, but the organelle retained a reduced genome and the bacterial-type membrane machinery still visible in mitochondria today. The result is a single eukaryotic genome that is a mosaic: archaeal in its core machinery, bacterial in select metabolic and membrane-related genes.

Applying this concept as a problem-solving tool means using it to interpret genomic data. Suppose a biologist sequences a newly discovered single-celled organism and finds that its ribosomal proteins cluster genetically with archaea in a phylogenetic tree, while its lipid-synthesis and electron-transport genes cluster with a bacterial group. The genome fusion model predicts exactly this pattern and lets the researcher generate a testable hypothesis: this organism's ancestors underwent an endosymbiotic fusion event, and the organism may possess an organelle (or organelle remnant) derived from the bacterial partner. This is precisely the reasoning that led researchers to identify Asgard archaea as the closest known archaeal relatives of eukaryotes — by systematically sorting eukaryotic genes by phylogenetic affinity and recognizing the two-source, chimeric signature. Genome fusion thus reframes eukaryotic ancestry not as a simple branching tree but as a network event, where a single node results from the joining of two prior branches — a distinction that changes how biologists build and interpret the tree of life itself.
