Receptor Dimerization

Definition. Many cell-surface receptors do not activate as single molecules. Instead, they must pair up — either with an identical partner or a different receptor type — before the part of the protein inside the cell can send a signal. This pairing is called dimerization. Binding of a signaling molecule (a ligand, such as a growth factor or hormone) to the outer part of the receptor typically triggers or stabilizes this pairing. Once paired, the two inner portions of the receptors are brought close enough together to modify each other chemically, most commonly by attaching phosphate groups to specific sites. This mutual activation is the switch that starts the signaling cascade inside the cell.

Worked example. Consider the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), found in the membranes of many cell types. In its resting state, EGFR molecules diffuse independently in the membrane, unpaired and inactive. When epidermal growth factor (EGF) binds the outer region of one receptor, it causes a shape change that exposes a contact surface. Two ligand-bound receptors then latch onto each other at these surfaces, forming a stable pair. This physical pairing positions the two inner enzyme domains so that each one attaches phosphate groups to specific sites on its partner. The phosphorylated sites then recruit other signaling proteins and launch pathways that regulate cell growth and division.

Problem-solving application. Dimerization is a control point that cells and drugs alike can exploit. Suppose a tumor cell overexpresses EGFR, so receptors are packed densely enough on the membrane that pairs form spontaneously — without EGF, and without any external growth signal. This spontaneous pairing continuously activates the growth pathway, driving uncontrolled proliferation, and is a mechanism seen in several cancers. Knowing that pairing, not ligand binding alone, is the trigger for activation immediately suggests two distinct drug strategies: (1) block the ligand-binding site so EGF cannot dock in the first place, or (2) block the contact surface itself, preventing two receptors from pairing even if ligand is present. Monoclonal antibody drugs such as cetuximab work by the first strategy, while others target the pairing surface directly. Distinguishing which step of the pathway a therapy interrupts — ligand binding versus pairing versus the enzyme activity that follows — is essential for predicting which mutations will make a tumor resistant to a given drug.
