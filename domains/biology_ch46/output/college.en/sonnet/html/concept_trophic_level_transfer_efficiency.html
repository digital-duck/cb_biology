Trophic Level Transfer Efficiency

Every time energy moves from one trophic level to the next—from producers to herbivores, from herbivores to carnivores—most of it is lost, not passed on. Trophic level transfer efficiency (TLTE) measures the fraction of energy captured at one level that is successfully incorporated into the biomass of the level above it. Formally,

$$ \text{TLTE} = \frac{E_{n+1}}{E_n} \times 100\% $$

where $E_n$ is the energy (or biomass, as a proxy) stored at trophic level $n$, and $E_{n+1}$ is the energy stored at the next level up. Ecologists commonly cite an average value near 10%, though real ecosystems range from about 5% to 20% depending on the organisms involved.

The loss is not mysterious—it follows directly from the second law of thermodynamics, which says that whenever energy is used to do work, some of it is inevitably lost as heat rather than passed on unchanged. At each trophic level, organisms use captured energy for respiration, movement, growth, and reproduction, and most of that energy ultimately dissipates as heat. Additional energy is lost as uneaten biomass, indigestible material, and waste. Only what is converted into new consumer biomass counts as "transferred."

Worked example. Suppose a wetland has producers (algae and plants) fixing $20{,}000\ \text{kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}$ of solar energy into biomass. If the transfer efficiency to primary consumers (zooplankton, insects) is 10%, then those consumers gain $20{,}000 \times 0.10 = 2{,}000\ \text{kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}$. If secondary consumers (small fish) also operate at 10% efficiency, they gain $2{,}000 \times 0.10 = 200\ \text{kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}$. A top predator (a heron) feeding on those fish, again at 10%, gains only $20\ \text{kcal/m}^2/\text{yr}$—a thousandfold reduction from the original solar energy captured by producers.

Problem-solving application. TLTE explains two practical patterns you can use to reason about real ecosystems. First, it sets an upper bound on food-chain length: energy losses compound so severely that chains rarely exceed four or five levels, since there is simply too little energy left to support another level. Second, it explains why agriculture is more efficient when humans eat plants directly rather than eating animals that ate plants: each additional trophic step multiplies losses. Given known efficiencies, you can estimate how much land, feed, or primary production is required to sustain a population at a given diet—useful for comparing the ecological footprint of, say, a vegetarian diet versus a diet reliant on grain-fed livestock.
