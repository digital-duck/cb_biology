Diploid Dominant Life Cycle

Definition. In a diploid-dominant life cycle, the organism spends nearly its entire existence as a multicellular diploid ($2n$) body — every cell carries two full sets of chromosomes, one from each parent. The only haploid ($n$) stage is exceptionally brief and restricted to a single cell type: gametes (sperm and egg). These gametes are not produced directly by ordinary body cells; instead, specialized germ cells located in gonads (testes and ovaries) undergo meiosis to reduce their chromosome number from $2n$ to $n$. This is the life-cycle pattern used by essentially all animals, including humans. Contrast this with plants and many algae, which spend significant time as both a multicellular haploid organism and a multicellular diploid one — a different pattern covered elsewhere.

Worked example. Consider a human male, whose diploid body cells contain 46 chromosomes ($2n = 46$). Within the testes, diploid germ cells undergo meiosis, producing four haploid sperm cells, each with 23 chromosomes ($n = 23$). Similarly, in the ovaries, a diploid germ cell undergoes meiosis to yield a haploid egg with 23 chromosomes. When sperm ($n=23$) fertilizes egg ($n=23$), the resulting zygote is restored to the diploid number ($2n = 46$), and this single diploid cell then divides to build the entire multicellular body. Note that the haploid phase here lasts only from gamete formation to fertilization — often minutes to days — while the diploid phase spans the organism's entire lifetime.

Problem-solving application. This pattern lets you predict chromosome numbers at any stage without memorizing species-specific facts, as long as you know the diploid number and the location of meiosis. Suppose a species has $2n = 8$. A biologist samples cells from three sources: skin tissue, a mature sperm cell, and a fertilized egg. You can immediately assign chromosome counts: skin tissue (body cell, diploid) = 8; mature sperm (gamete) = 4; fertilized egg (post-fusion) = 8. The key diagnostic question to apply in any unfamiliar case is: "Is this cell a gamete formed by meiosis in a gonad, or is it any other cell in the body?" If the former, halve the diploid number; if the latter, the diploid number applies unchanged. This single rule captures what makes diploid-dominant animals distinctive — the haploid state is confined to gametes alone, never to a multicellular stage of life.
