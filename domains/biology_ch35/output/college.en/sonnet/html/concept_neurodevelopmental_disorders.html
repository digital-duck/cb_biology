Neurodevelopmental Disorders

Neurodevelopmental disorders are conditions that arise when the processes shaping the nervous system before and after birth — neuron migration, synapse formation, synaptic pruning, and myelination — go awry. Because these processes unfold on a predictable timeline, the resulting disorders typically appear in infancy or childhood and affect how the brain organizes itself for learning, communication, movement, or social behavior. Examples include autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), intellectual disability, and specific learning disorders such as dyslexia. Unlike degenerative diseases, which involve loss of previously functioning tissue, neurodevelopmental disorders reflect atypical construction of neural circuits from the start.

A useful worked example is synaptic pruning, the process by which the brain eliminates weak or redundant synapses during childhood and adolescence to refine efficient circuits — roughly half of a toddler's synapses are eliminated by adulthood. In typical development, pruning follows an experience-dependent rule: synapses that are used are stabilized, and those that are not are removed, a principle summarized by the phrase "cells that fire together, wire together." In autism spectrum disorder, postmortem and imaging studies show reduced pruning, leaving an unusually high density of synapses in some cortical regions; this excess connectivity is thought to impair the signal-to-noise ratio of neural processing. In schizophrenia, by contrast, evidence points to excessive pruning during adolescence, particularly in prefrontal cortex, which may explain why symptoms often emerge in the late teens. The same mechanism, miscalibrated in opposite directions, produces different clinical pictures.

For problem-solving, a clinician or researcher evaluating a neurodevelopmental disorder should reason through three linked questions: (1) Which developmental process is disrupted — migration, synaptogenesis, pruning, or myelination? (2) What is the expected timing of that process, and does the age of symptom onset match it? (3) What functional systems depend on the affected circuitry, and do the observed symptoms map onto those systems? For instance, if a child shows movement and cortical-organization deficits present from birth, migration disorders should be suspected; if symptoms emerge specifically around adolescence, pruning-related processes are a more plausible target. This timeline-matching approach converts a broad diagnostic category into a testable hypothesis about which developmental mechanism to investigate further.
