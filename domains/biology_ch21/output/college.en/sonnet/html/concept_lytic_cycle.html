Lytic Cycle

The lytic cycle is a mode of viral reproduction in which a virus hijacks a host cell's machinery to manufacture many new virus particles (virions), then destroys the cell by rupturing its membrane — a process called lysis — to release them. It is one of two major replication strategies used by viruses that infect bacteria (bacteriophages) and many viruses that infect animal cells; the alternative, the lysogenic cycle, inserts viral DNA into the host genome and remains dormant instead of immediately destroying the cell.

The cycle proceeds through five stages. First, attachment: the virus binds to specific receptor molecules on the host cell surface — a step so specific that it largely determines which species and cell types a virus can infect. Second, penetration: the virus injects its genetic material into the cell (bacteriophages inject DNA through the cell wall while leaving the protein coat outside; many animal viruses are engulfed whole). Third, biosynthesis: the host's ribosomes, enzymes, and nucleotides are redirected to replicate viral genetic material and synthesize viral proteins — the host's own gene expression is typically shut down. Fourth, assembly: newly made genomes and protein coats self-assemble into complete virions. Fifth, lysis and release: viral enzymes (such as bacteriophage lysozyme) degrade the host cell wall or membrane, the cell bursts, and dozens to hundreds of new virions are released to infect neighboring cells.

Worked example. Suppose a bacteriophage completes one lytic cycle in 30 minutes and each infected cell releases a burst size of 100 new virions. Starting from a single infected bacterium in a culture, after one cycle you have 100 virions; if each of those successfully infects a new cell and completes another cycle 30 minutes later, you get $100 \times 100 = 10{,}000$ virions after one hour, and $100^n$ after $n$ cycles (assuming an unlimited supply of host cells — a simplification, since real infections are eventually limited by host availability and immune response). This exponential growth is why bacteriophage or viral outbreaks can escalate so rapidly once lytic replication begins.

Application. This exponential logic underlies real problem-solving: virologists estimate burst size and cycle time experimentally (via one-step growth curves) to predict outbreak dynamics, and phage therapy researchers exploit the lytic cycle deliberately, selecting lytic-only phages (never lysogenic ones) as antibacterial agents so that target bacteria are reliably destroyed rather than potentially protected by an integrated, dormant prophage.
