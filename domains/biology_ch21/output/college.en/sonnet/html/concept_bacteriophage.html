Bacteriophage

A bacteriophage (or "phage") is a virus that infects bacteria. Like all viruses, it cannot reproduce on its own — it hijacks a host cell's machinery to copy its genetic material and build new virus particles. Many phages have a distinctive structure: an icosahedral (many-sided) protein head that stores the viral genome, connected to a tail made of a contractile sheath, base plate, and tail fibers. The tail fibers recognize and bind to specific receptor molecules on the bacterial surface, and the sheath contracts like a syringe, driving a hollow tube through the bacterial cell wall so the genome can be injected directly into the cytoplasm — the capsid itself stays outside.

Worked example. Consider T4 phage infecting E. coli. After injection, the phage genome redirects the bacterial ribosomes and polymerases to transcribe and translate phage genes instead of the host's own. Within about 20–30 minutes, the cell has assembled roughly 100–200 new phage particles, at which point a phage-encoded enzyme (lysozyme) degrades the bacterial cell wall from the inside, lysing the cell and releasing the new phages to infect neighboring bacteria. This is called the lytic cycle. Some phages instead follow a lysogenic cycle: their DNA integrates into the host chromosome and is copied passively along with the bacterium's own DNA for many generations before eventually switching to lytic replication.

Problem-solving application. Because phages are extremely host-specific — a given phage typically infects only one bacterial species or even a narrow strain, due to the exact match required between tail fibers and surface receptors — they are being explored as targeted antibacterial therapies ("phage therapy"), especially against antibiotic-resistant infections. Suppose a patient has a wound infection and a lab identifies the bacterial strain by testing which phages successfully lyse it in culture (a "phage susceptibility test"). A clinician could then select a phage cocktail matched to that strain, killing the pathogen while leaving beneficial gut bacteria (which have different receptors) unaffected — a specificity advantage broad-spectrum antibiotics lack. Phages are also indispensable laboratory tools: because they replicate and package DNA with high fidelity, molecular biologists use phage vectors to clone and deliver genes into bacterial cells, and phage-display techniques exploit their surface proteins to screen for antibody or peptide candidates in drug discovery.
