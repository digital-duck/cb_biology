Reverse Transcriptase

Definition. Reverse transcriptase (RT) is an enzyme that synthesizes DNA using an RNA template — the reverse of the normal transcription direction (DNA → RNA), hence the name. It is encoded by retroviruses (Baltimore Group VI, such as HIV) and by pararetroviruses like hepatitis B (Group VII), both of which route part of their replication cycle through this RNA-to-DNA step. No uninfected human or animal cell performs this reaction as part of its normal biology, which makes RT both a hallmark of retroviral infection and a highly selective drug target: a molecule that blocks RT should have little effect on the host's own enzymes.

Worked example. Consider HIV, whose genome is single-stranded RNA. After the virus fuses with a host T-cell and releases its contents into the cytoplasm, RT performs three linked jobs on that RNA: (1) it uses the viral RNA as a template to synthesize a complementary DNA (cDNA) strand, forming an RNA-DNA hybrid; (2) an RNase H domain of the same enzyme degrades the RNA strand of that hybrid; (3) RT then uses the single-stranded cDNA as a template to synthesize a second DNA strand, producing double-stranded DNA. This DNA is subsequently integrated into the host genome by a separate viral enzyme, integrase, and from there it can be transcribed by the host's own RNA polymerase — permanently embedding the virus's genetic information in the cell.

Problem-solving application. Because RT is unique to the virus and essential for converting its RNA genome into an integratable form, it is the direct target of an entire class of antiretroviral drugs: nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs, e.g., zidovudine) and non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs). NRTIs work by mimicking the natural building blocks (nucleotides) RT needs; once incorporated into the growing DNA strand, they lack the chemical group required to add the next nucleotide, so DNA synthesis halts — a strategy called chain termination. If you were designing a diagnostic or therapeutic strategy against a newly discovered RNA virus, the presence or absence of a reverse transcriptase step in its Baltimore classification tells you immediately whether RT inhibitors are even a viable option: a Group IV virus (positive-sense RNA replicated directly by an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase) would not respond to an RT inhibitor, whereas a Group VI virus would.
