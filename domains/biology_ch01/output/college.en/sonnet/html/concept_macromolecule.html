Macromolecule

A macromolecule is a large molecule built from repeating structural units called monomers, linked together through covalent bonds in a process called polymerization. The resulting polymer can range from a few hundred to millions of daltons in molecular weight. Biology recognizes four major classes of macromolecules: carbohydrates (built from monosaccharide monomers), proteins (built from amino acids), nucleic acids (built from nucleotides), and lipids, which are technically not true polymers since they lack a repeating monomer-linkage pattern but are grouped with macromolecules due to their size and biological role.

The chemistry behind assembling most macromolecules is dehydration synthesis (also called a condensation reaction): each time two monomers join, a water molecule is released, and a new covalent bond — a glycosidic bond in carbohydrates, a peptide bond in proteins, a phosphodiester bond in nucleic acids — forms between them. The reverse process, hydrolysis, breaks these bonds by adding water back, which is how digestion disassembles dietary macromolecules into absorbable monomers.

Consider glucose polymerizing into starch. Each glucose unit joins the growing chain by losing an –OH from one carbon and an –H from the neighboring glucose's –OH group, releasing H₂O and forming an α-1,4-glycosidic bond. Repeat this thousands of times and you get amylose, a storage polysaccharide. The same monomer, glucose, linked with a different bond geometry (β-1,4) produces cellulose — a structural polysaccharide that most animals cannot digest because they lack the enzyme to cleave that particular bond. This illustrates a key problem-solving principle: a macromolecule's function is determined not just by which monomers compose it, but by the specific bond type and three-dimensional arrangement linking them.

This principle has direct diagnostic value. If you're given an unknown macromolecule and told it is broken down by amylase but not cellulase, you can infer it contains α-linkages, not β-linkages, even without seeing its full structure. Similarly, when analyzing a protein's function, you must look beyond "it's made of amino acids" to the specific sequence and resulting folded shape, since a single amino acid substitution (as in sickle-cell hemoglobin) can alter an entire macromolecule's behavior despite negligible change in composition.
