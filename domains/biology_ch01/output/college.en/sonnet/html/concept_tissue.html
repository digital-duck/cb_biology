Tissue

A tissue is a group of similar cells, along with the extracellular material surrounding them, that work together to perform a shared function. Cells rarely act alone: a single muscle cell contracting does little, but billions of them organized into muscle tissue can move a whole limb. Biologists classify animal tissues into four fundamental types — epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous — each defined by the shape of its cells, how those cells are arranged, and the job they carry out.

Worked example. Consider the wall of the small intestine, a structure built from all four tissue types layered together. The innermost lining is epithelial tissue: tightly packed cells arranged in a continuous sheet that controls what gets absorbed into the bloodstream and what stays in the gut. Beneath it lies connective tissue, which is sparser in cells but rich in fibers and fluid — it binds the epithelial layer to the muscle beneath and carries blood vessels for nutrient transport. Surrounding that is smooth muscle tissue, whose elongated cells contract in coordinated waves (peristalsis) to push food along. Finally, nervous tissue threads through the wall, its neurons detecting stretch and triggering the muscle contractions. No single tissue could digest food on its own; the function emerges from their coordination, which is why the next level of organization — the organ — is built from tissues rather than from cells directly.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a researcher examines a tissue sample and needs to classify it. The diagnostic approach is systematic: first, count how much extracellular material separates the cells. Epithelial tissue has almost none (cells touch); connective tissue has abundant material relative to its cells. Second, check for elongated, contractile cells — a hallmark of muscle. Third, look for cells with long, thin projections used for signaling — nervous tissue. Applying this logic, if a sample shows cells packed edge-to-edge in a single sheet with a free surface facing a cavity, it is almost certainly epithelial — likely part of a lining, such as skin or the gut wall. This kind of reasoning is exactly how pathologists identify unhealthy tissue: a biopsy showing epithelial cells that have lost their orderly sheet-like arrangement and are instead growing in disorganized clusters is a warning sign, since normal tissue architecture reflects a specific, functional relationship between cells that disease often disrupts first.
