Science

Science is a systematic way of acquiring and testing knowledge about the natural world. What sets it apart from other kinds of knowledge is not the subject matter but the method: observations lead to a proposed explanation, called a hypothesis, and that hypothesis is only accepted as reliable knowledge once it survives repeated, deliberate attempts to prove it wrong. A claim that cannot in principle be tested or shown false — no matter how plausible it sounds — falls outside the scope of science.

Worked example. Suppose a biologist notices that plants near a factory grow more slowly than plants farther away. A hypothesis might be: "Airborne pollutant X reduces plant growth rate." To test this, the biologist sets up two groups of genetically similar seedlings, grown under identical light, water, and soil conditions, except that one group is exposed to pollutant X and the other is not. Keeping everything the same except the one factor being tested is what makes the comparison meaningful — without it, any difference in growth could be blamed on something else entirely. After four weeks, average stem height and leaf count are measured and compared. If the exposed group grows significantly less, and the result holds up when other researchers repeat the experiment, the hypothesis gains support. If it does not hold up, the hypothesis must be revised or discarded. The design is set up so the hypothesis could fail — that possibility of failure is what makes it a scientific claim rather than an opinion.

Problem-solving application. In practice, applying this method means being able to diagnose flawed studies. Consider a headline claiming "a new supplement improves memory," based on a study with no comparison group, ten participants, and a single trial. As a critical reader, you should ask: What was this outcome actually compared against? Could the improvement have shown up anyway, just by chance or because participants expected to do better? Have other researchers gotten the same result when they tried it again? This kind of evaluation — noticing when a study has nothing to compare against, too few participants, or a claim that no result could ever contradict — is the practical skill that distinguishes scientific literacy from simply memorizing facts. Science, then, is less a fixed body of facts than an ongoing process of proposing explanations and rigorously trying to break them; the facts that survive that process become the reliable knowledge a discipline builds on.
