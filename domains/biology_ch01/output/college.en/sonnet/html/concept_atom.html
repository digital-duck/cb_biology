Atom

An atom is the smallest unit of matter that retains the chemical identity of an element. Every atom consists of a dense central nucleus, made of positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged electrons. The number of protons — the atomic number — defines which element the atom is: an atom with 6 protons is always carbon, whether it has 6, 7, or 8 neutrons (giving carbon-12, carbon-13, or carbon-14, its isotopes). Because protons and electrons carry equal and opposite charges, a neutral atom has the same number of each; gaining or losing electrons produces a charged ion instead.

Worked example. Consider a neutral atom of oxygen, atomic number 8. It has 8 protons and 8 electrons. If its mass number (protons + neutrons) is 16, it has 8 neutrons — this is the common isotope oxygen-16. Now suppose the same atom gains two electrons, forming the oxide ion, $\text{O}^{2-}$. It still has 8 protons (still oxygen), but now 10 electrons, giving it a net charge of $-2$. Note that gaining or losing electrons never changes which element the atom is — only proton number does that.

Problem-solving application. Atomic structure lets you predict an atom's behavior directly from the periodic table. Suppose you're told an atom has 17 protons, 18 neutrons, and 18 electrons. First, identify the element from the proton count: 17 protons corresponds to chlorine (Cl). Second, calculate its mass number: $17 + 18 = 35$, so it's the isotope chlorine-35. Third, compare protons to electrons: 17 protons but 18 electrons means one extra negative charge, so this is the chloride ion, $\text{Cl}^-$ — the same ion found in table salt (NaCl).

This kind of reasoning — using proton count to fix identity, neutron count to determine the isotope, and the proton–electron balance to determine charge — is the basic toolkit for interpreting any atomic notation you'll encounter in chemistry, from isotope labels used in radiometric dating to the ions that drive nerve signaling in biology.
