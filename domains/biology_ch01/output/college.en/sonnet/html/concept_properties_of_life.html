Properties Of Life

Biologists recognize that no single trait uniquely identifies life; instead, living organisms share a cluster of eight interlocking characteristics. An entity is considered alive if it exhibits: (1) cellular organization — composed of one or more cells; (2) metabolism — the chemical processes that convert energy and matter to sustain the organism; (3) homeostasis — maintaining stable internal conditions despite external change; (4) growth and development — an increase in size or complexity following a genetically directed pattern; (5) reproduction — producing offspring, whether asexually or sexually; (6) response to stimuli — detecting and reacting to environmental change; (7) heredity — transmitting genetic information via DNA or RNA; and (8) evolutionary adaptation — populations changing over generations in response to selective pressures.

A virus illustrates why all eight properties matter together, not individually. A virus has genetic material (heredity) and can evolve, but it has no cells, no metabolism of its own, and cannot reproduce without hijacking a host cell's machinery. Because it fails several criteria, biologists classify viruses as non-living particles rather than organisms — even though they blur the boundary. Compare this to a bacterium: it maintains its own metabolism, regulates its internal chemistry (homeostasis), grows, divides (reproduction), responds to nutrients or toxins, and adapts across generations via mutation and selection. It satisfies all eight, so it is unambiguously alive.

This checklist approach is a genuine problem-solving tool, not just a definition to memorize. Suppose you're handed a description of an unfamiliar entity — say, a synthetic self-replicating nanostructure that copies itself using an external energy source but has no internal metabolic regulation and cannot evolve independently. Working through the list systematically: does it have cells? Does it regulate its own internal state? Can its lineage undergo heritable variation and selection? Answering these questions one by one, rather than relying on intuition, is exactly how biologists resolve edge cases like viruses, prions, and even hypothetical astrobiology samples returned from other planets.

This same diagnostic habit — testing a candidate against a fixed set of necessary criteria rather than a single defining feature — reappears throughout biology and other sciences whenever a category has fuzzy boundaries, such as classifying a species or determining whether a chemical reaction is truly autocatalytic.
