Community

A community is a group of populations of different species living and interacting in the same place at the same time. Where a population is one species and an ecosystem includes the nonliving environment, community sits in between: it is the biological neighborhood — every species present and every relationship among them, from predation and competition to pollination and decomposition. Communities are described by two related properties: species richness (the number of different species present) and species diversity (richness combined with how evenly individuals are distributed among those species). A forest with ten species, each represented by roughly equal numbers, is more diverse than a forest with the same ten species where one dominates 90% of the individuals — even though richness is identical in both.

Worked example. Suppose two ponds each contain 100 individual fish across 4 species. Pond A has 25 individuals of each species. Pond B has 94, 2, 2, and 2. Richness is identical (4 species), but diversity differs sharply. Using the Shannon diversity index, $H = -\sum_{i=1}^{S} p_i \ln p_i$, where $p_i$ is the proportion of individuals belonging to species $i$: for Pond A, $p_i = 0.25$ for each species, giving $H = -4(0.25 \ln 0.25) \approx 1.386$. For Pond B, computing each term ($p=0.94$: $-0.94\ln0.94 \approx 0.0565$; three terms at $p=0.02$: each $-0.02\ln0.02 \approx 0.0782$) sums to $H \approx 0.291$. Pond A's community is far more diverse, even though both have 4 species and 100 fish.

Problem-solving application. Ecologists use diversity indices like this to detect environmental stress before it's visually obvious. A sudden drop in $H$ over successive years — even without any species disappearing — signals that one species is becoming dominant, often because a competitor, predator, or resource limitation has changed. If you're given population counts before and after a disturbance (a chemical spill, invasive species introduction, or habitat fragmentation), you can calculate $H$ for each time point and quantify the community's response, rather than relying on the vaguer claim that "biodiversity decreased." This turns community ecology into a measurable, comparable science.
