Strobilus

A strobilus (plural: strobili), commonly called a cone, is a compact, spiral or whorled arrangement of modified leaves — sporophylls — packed tightly around a central axis. In gymnosperms such as pines, spruces, and cycads, strobili are the structures that house the reproductive machinery: pollen-producing microsporophylls in male cones or seed-bearing megasporophylls in female cones. The tight, overlapping arrangement is not decorative — it protects developing spores and, later, seeds and pollen from desiccation, herbivory, and mechanical damage while they mature.

Worked example. Consider a pine tree, which produces two distinct types of strobili on the same plant. Small, soft male cones cluster near the branch tips in spring; each scale (microsporophyll) carries pollen sacs (microsporangia) that release wind-dispersed pollen. Once pollen is shed, the male cones wither and drop — their job is done in weeks. Female cones, by contrast, are larger, woodier, and long-lived. Each of their scales (megasporophylls) bears two ovules on its upper surface. After pollination, fertilization is delayed for up to a year while the ovule develops into a seed; the cone scales stay tightly closed to protect the developing embryo, then dry out and separate to release seeds when mature. If you compare a fresh male cone to a two-year-old female cone from the same tree, the difference in size, texture, and rigidity directly reflects this difference in function and time scale.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you find a cone-like structure and must decide whether it is a true strobilus or a superficially similar structure, such as a flower cluster or a fruit. Ask three diagnostic questions: (1) Are the scales themselves modified leaves attached directly to a central axis, rather than parts derived from a flower (petals, sepals, carpels)? (2) Does the structure lack any accessory tissue such as a fleshy fruit wall, since gymnosperm seeds are "naked" and not enclosed in an ovary? (3) Is the plant a conifer, cycad, or related gymnosperm rather than a flowering plant? A "yes" to all three confirms a true strobilus. This reasoning matters practically for foresters and botanists identifying species by cone morphology — scale shape, size, and arrangement are often the most reliable field characters for distinguishing closely related conifer species.
