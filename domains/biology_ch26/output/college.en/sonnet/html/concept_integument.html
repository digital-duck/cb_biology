Integument

The integument is the layer of sporophyte (diploid parent plant) tissue that surrounds the megasporangium — the structure inside which the female gametophyte and egg cell develop. In flowering plants and most gymnosperms, the integument does not enclose the megasporangium completely; it leaves a small opening called the micropyle, through which a pollen tube can later enter to deliver sperm cells to the egg. After fertilization, the integument does not disappear — it hardens and transforms into the seed coat, the protective outer shell of the mature seed.

Consider a garden pea flower as a worked example. Inside each ovule (the structure that will become a seed), a megasporangium is wrapped in integument tissue, with the micropyle left open at one end. When a pollen grain lands on the stigma, it germinates and grows a pollen tube down through the style, into the ovary, and directly through the micropyle. Sperm cells travel through this tube to reach the egg inside. Once fertilization is complete, the ovule matures into a seed: the embryo develops inside, and the integument thickens and dries out, becoming the tough, often brown or speckled pea seed coat you can peel off a soaked pea seed with your fingernail.

Why does this structure matter for problem-solving in plant biology? Seed coat traits are one of the most common criteria breeders and seed scientists use to predict germination behavior, because the integument's structure directly determines it. A thick, waxy integument-derived seed coat (as in many legumes) can cause "hard seededness," where water cannot penetrate and germination is delayed for months or years until the coat is scarified — scratched or weakened — by weathering, digestion, or fire. Conversely, a thin integument produces a seed coat that imbibes water quickly and germinates readily but offers less protection from pathogens or desiccation. So if you are asked to explain why one crop variety germinates unevenly in the field while another germinates uniformly within days, the integument's thickness and permeability — inherited from the sporophyte parent — is often the first structural feature to examine, and dictates whether a pretreatment such as mechanical or chemical scarification is needed before planting.
