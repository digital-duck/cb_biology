Gnetophyte

Definition. Gnetophytes are a small, evolutionarily peculiar phylum of gymnosperms comprising three living genera — Ephedra (desert shrubs), Gnetum (tropical vines and trees), and Welwitschia (a bizarre two-leaved plant of the Namib Desert). Despite their strikingly different appearances, all three share a diagnostic anatomical trait: their xylem contains vessel elements, the wide, efficient water-conducting cells otherwise considered a hallmark of flowering plants (angiosperms). Most gymnosperms — conifers, cycads, ginkgo — transport water only through narrower, less efficient cells called tracheids. Gnetophytes also show other angiosperm-like features, including flower-like reproductive structures and double fertilization in some species, which for decades fueled the hypothesis that they were the closest living relatives of angiosperms.

Worked example. Consider a botanist comparing wood samples from a pine tree (a conifer) and from Gnetum gnemon. Under the microscope, the pine wood shows only tracheids: elongated cells with tapered ends and pitted walls, each connected to its neighbor through narrow pits that restrict water flow. The Gnetum sample additionally shows vessel elements: shorter, wider cells stacked end-to-end with perforated end walls (perforation plates) that create an open pipeline. This is functionally identical to what she'd see in oak or maple wood. If vessel elements are a derived trait that evolved independently in gnetophytes and angiosperms, rather than inherited from a shared vessel-bearing ancestor, this is a case of convergent evolution — the same solution to the same problem (fast water transport) arising twice.

Problem-solving application. This is precisely the puzzle systematists faced: does anatomical similarity mean common ancestry, or convergence? The answer requires distinguishing homology from homoplasy using independent evidence. When molecular phylogenetics (comparing DNA sequences across many genes) became available, it placed gnetophytes not as sister to angiosperms but nested among the conifers — meaning vessel elements evolved separately in the two lineages. The practical lesson: a single shared trait, however striking, is weak evidence for relatedness if it could plausibly evolve twice under similar selective pressure. Robust classification requires triangulating multiple independent characters — morphological, developmental, and molecular — rather than resting on one convenient similarity.
