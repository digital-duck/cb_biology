Seed

A seed is a complete biological package for starting the next generation of a plant. It forms after fertilization of an ovule and consists of three parts working together: an embryo (the miniature plant-to-be, with rudimentary root and shoot already differentiated), a nutrient storage tissue (endosperm, or in some species the swollen cotyledons themselves) that fuels early growth before photosynthesis begins, and a protective seed coat derived from the ovule's outer layers, which guards against desiccation, mechanical damage, and premature germination.

Worked example. Consider a bean seed. Crack one open (after soaking it in water) and you can identify all three structures with the naked eye: the seed coat peels away as a thin papery layer; inside, two thick cotyledons make up most of the mass — these are the nutrient reserve; and nestled at one end is the tiny embryonic axis, with a plumule (future shoot) and radicle (future root) already visible. The reason the bean can be dried, stored in a jar for years, and still sprout when planted is precisely this three-part design: the coat prevents water loss and pathogen entry during dormancy, while the cotyledons hold enough starch and protein to power the seedling until its first leaves emerge and photosynthesis takes over.

Problem-solving application. Seeds solve two problems that a plant, being immobile as an adult, cannot solve any other way: dispersal through space and dormancy through time. Suppose you are a farmer or plant breeder deciding when to plant stored seed, or an ecologist explaining why a desert can erupt into bloom after years of drought the moment rain finally falls. In both cases, the underlying logic is the same: the seed coat's impermeability and the embryo's suspended metabolism let the organism "wait out" unfavorable conditions — cold, drought, or lack of a suitable dispersal opportunity — and germinate only when internal or environmental cues (adequate moisture, temperature, sometimes fire or passage through an animal gut) signal that conditions favor seedling survival. Recognizing dormancy as an adaptive timing strategy, not passive inactivity, lets you predict why certain seeds require scarification, cold stratification, or a period of after-ripening before they will germinate at all.
