Ginkgophyte

Ginkgophyta is a phylum of seed-bearing gymnosperms that today contains exactly one living species, Ginkgo biloba, often called a "living fossil" because its fossil relatives extend back over 200 million years to the Mesozoic era, while every other lineage in the phylum has gone extinct. Ginkgo produces seeds rather than flowers or fruit, does not undergo double fertilization, and is dioecious — meaning individual trees are either male or female, never both. Male trees produce pollen cones; female trees produce ovules that, once fertilized, develop into fleshy, foul-smelling seed coats surrounding a hard-shelled seed. The species is identified by its distinctive fan-shaped, dichotomously veined leaves, which turn brilliant yellow before dropping in autumn.

Worked example. Suppose a botanical garden wants to plant ginkgo trees along a walkway but avoid the odor problem caused by fertilized seeds from female trees. Applying the concept of dioecy, the correct solution is to plant only male ginkgo trees, since without a female tree nearby, no seeds — and therefore no smell — will be produced. Nurseries solve this in practice by propagating male trees from cuttings or grafts rather than growing them from seed, because seedlings cannot be sexed until they mature and flower after 20–30 years; cloning guarantees the sex of the resulting tree.

Problem-solving application. This case illustrates a broader principle in plant biology: separate-sexed (dioecious) species require two individuals in proximity for sexual reproduction, unlike the ~90% of flowering plant species that are monoecious or hermaphroditic and can self-pollinate or rely on a single individual. When designing conservation or landscaping strategies for dioecious species, you must account for population sex ratios — a stand of all-female or all-male ginkgo trees, however large, will never reproduce naturally. This same reasoning generalizes to other dioecious organisms, such as holly or date palms: successful cultivation or habitat restoration plans must verify that both sexes are represented in sufficient numbers and proximity, since genetic isolation of a single sex is functionally equivalent to sterility for the population as a whole.
