Heterospory

Most familiar plants—ferns, mosses, and their relatives—are homosporous: they produce a single kind of spore that grows into a gametophyte bearing both male and female sex organs. Heterospory is different. A heterosporous sporophyte produces two structurally and functionally distinct spore types: megaspores, which are larger, fewer in number, and develop into female gametophytes that produce eggs; and microspores, which are smaller, far more numerous, and develop into male gametophytes that produce sperm. This dual production system is a defining trait of all seed plants (gymnosperms and angiosperms) and also appears in a few seedless lineages, such as the spikemosses (Selaginella) and quillworts (Isoetes), making it a useful marker for tracing the evolutionary steps toward the seed habit.

Worked example. Consider a pine tree, a classic seed plant. Its female cones house ovules where a single diploid cell undergoes meiosis to produce four megaspores, but typically only one survives and divides repeatedly to form a multicellular female gametophyte containing the egg. Meanwhile, the male cones contain structures where similar mother cells undergo meiosis to produce vast numbers of microspores. Each microspore develops into a pollen grain—the male gametophyte—which is released and transported (by wind, in pines) to the ovule. This size and number asymmetry is functional: the female gametophyte needs stored resources to nourish the future embryo, favoring a large, resource-rich, low-number strategy, while the male gametophyte only needs to deliver sperm efficiently, favoring a small, high-number, easily dispersed strategy.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you are given an unidentified plant specimen and must determine whether it is homosporous or heterosporous using only a light microscope. Your strategy: examine the spore-producing structures for two visibly different types and two correspondingly different spore sizes. If you see uniform spores of one size produced in one type of structure, the species is homosporous. If you find a size dimorphism—many small spores from one structure and few large spores from another—you can classify it as heterosporous. This diagnostic approach is exactly how botanists reconstruct evolutionary relationships in the fossil record, since spore size and the architecture of the structures that produced them often survive as identifiable fossils long after other soft tissues decay.
