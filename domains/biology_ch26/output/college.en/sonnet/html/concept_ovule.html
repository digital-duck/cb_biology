Ovule

The ovule is the structure inside a flowering plant's ovary where the female gametophyte develops and where, after fertilization, the seed is formed. Each ovule consists of a mass of tissue called the nucellus, which contains the megasporangium — the site of megaspore production through meiosis. Surrounding the nucellus are one or two protective layers called integuments, which leave a small opening, the micropyle, through which the pollen tube eventually enters. The ovule attaches to the ovary wall by a stalk called the funiculus.

Development begins when a diploid megasporocyte within the nucellus undergoes meiosis, producing four haploid megaspores. In most flowering plants, three of these degenerate, and the surviving one undergoes three rounds of mitosis without cell division, producing an eight-nucleate, seven-celled structure called the embryo sac — the female gametophyte. This embryo sac contains the egg cell, two synergid cells flanking it, three antipodal cells at the opposite end, and a central cell with two polar nuclei.

Consider a lily ovary containing 40 ovules, each undergoing this process independently. If pollination and fertilization succeed in every ovule, the plant will produce a fruit with up to 40 seeds, since each ovule matures into one seed following double fertilization: one sperm nucleus fuses with the egg to form the diploid zygote, while a second sperm nucleus fuses with the two polar nuclei to form the triploid endosperm, the seed's nutritive tissue. If only 25 ovules are successfully fertilized — due to insufficient pollen tube growth or incompatible pollen — the resulting fruit will contain only 25 seeds, with the remaining 15 ovules aborting.

This connects directly to agricultural problem-solving: a farmer noticing fewer seeds per fruit than expected should investigate factors affecting the ovule-to-seed conversion rate — pollinator visitation frequency, pollen viability, or resource limitation restricting how many fertilized ovules the parent plant can support to maturity. Because seed number often determines fruit size and market value (as in cucumbers or squash), understanding that each seed originates from an individual ovule allows growers to diagnose whether a poor harvest stems from pollination failure, fertilization failure, or later resource-driven seed abortion — each requiring a different intervention.
