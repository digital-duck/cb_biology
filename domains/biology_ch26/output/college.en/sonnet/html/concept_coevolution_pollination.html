Coevolution Pollination

Coevolution between flowering plants and their pollinators is a reciprocal evolutionary relationship in which each lineage exerts selective pressure on the other over many generations. A plant trait that attracts a pollinator more effectively — brighter color, stronger scent, a shape that fits a particular visitor — tends to increase seed production, so that trait spreads through the plant population. Meanwhile, any pollinator trait that lets an animal extract nectar or pollen more efficiently — a longer tongue, a better sense of smell, a body shape that matches a flower's geometry — improves that animal's food intake and reproductive success, so it spreads through the pollinator population. Because each side's fitness depends on the other's traits, the two populations can become locked into a feedback loop of mutual adaptation.

Worked example. Consider Darwin's orchid (Angraecum sesquipedale), native to Madagascar, which has a nectar spur roughly 30 cm long. Darwin predicted in 1862 that a moth with a proboscis long enough to reach the nectar at the bottom of the spur must exist, even though no such moth had yet been observed. Decades later, the moth Xanthopan morganii praedicta was found, with a proboscis matching the spur's length almost exactly. The logic: if the spur is too long for any existing pollinator, flowers with shorter spurs get pollinated more often and the population's average spur length should shrink — unless a moth with a long enough proboscis already exists or evolves to exploit it. The orchid's extreme spur length is evidence that a matching pollinator must have coevolved alongside it.

Problem-solving application. Use coevolutionary reasoning as a predictive tool: given a flower's morphology, you can infer the mechanical and sensory requirements of its likely pollinator, and vice versa. For instance, flowers pollinated by bats tend to be pale or white (visible at night), strongly scented, and open at dusk, while bird-pollinated flowers are often red (birds see red well, most insects do not), scentless (birds have a weak sense of smell), and tubular (matching a beak). When you encounter an unfamiliar flower or pollinator, list its measurable traits — color, scent, shape, bloom timing, reward quantity — and ask what selective pressure from a partner species would explain each one; this trait-by-trait inference is the core analytical skill coevolution problems require.
