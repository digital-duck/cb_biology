Seed Dispersal

Definition. Seed dispersal is the process by which a plant moves its offspring—seeds or fruits containing seeds—away from the location where they were produced. Because plants cannot walk, they rely on external vectors: wind (anemochory), water (hydrochory), or animals (zoochory), along with a few species that use explosive mechanical release (autochory). Dispersal matters ecologically for two reasons: it lets a species colonize new habitat and expand its range, and it reduces competition between parent and offspring for the same light, water, and soil nutrients. A seed that germinates directly beneath its parent competes with an established, larger organism it is unlikely to outcompete.

Worked example. Consider a maple tree, whose winged seeds (samaras) spin as they fall, and a cherry tree, whose fleshy fruit is eaten by birds that later excrete the seed elsewhere. Both solve the same problem—distance from the parent—with different engineering. The maple's wing increases air resistance relative to seed mass, slowing its fall so wind has more time to carry it laterally; this is a physical adaptation matched to an abiotic vector. The cherry instead invests energy into a sugar-rich fruit coat, a biological "payment" to a bird that provides transport, digestion-resistant seed coats, and often deposits the seed with a nutrient boost (the bird's droppings) far from the parent tree. Neither adaptation is superior in the abstract; each is suited to the vector available in that species' environment.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you are studying a coastal plant population and want to predict where colonization is most likely to succeed after a storm scatters seeds. Rather than treating dispersal as a single event, you can decompose the question into three factors: (1) which vector—wind, water, or an animal—is actually moving this species' seeds, (2) what dispersal distance is realistic for that vector (wind typically disperses tens of meters, ocean currents can carry buoyant seeds hundreds of kilometers, animal guts constrain distance to how far the animal ranges before defecating), and (3) whether the destination habitat matches the parent's requirements (soil, light, moisture). Applying this framework, you would predict that a coconut's water-dispersed, salt-tolerant seed is far more likely to establish on a new shoreline than a wind-dispersed inland species would be, because the vector distance and habitat match both favor it. This same three-factor approach—vector, distance, habitat match—is the standard way ecologists forecast range expansion or assess invasive species risk.
