Peristalsis

Peristalsis is the involuntary, wave-like contraction of smooth muscle lining the digestive tract that pushes food forward, from the esophagus down through the stomach, small intestine, and large intestine. The muscle wall of the gut is arranged in two layers: a circular layer wrapping around the tube and a longitudinal layer running along its length. Peristalsis works because these layers contract in coordinated sequence — circular muscle squeezes shut just behind a bolus of food while longitudinal muscle ahead of it relaxes and shortens, creating a moving zone of constriction that mechanically drives contents forward. This is regulated by the enteric nervous system, a semi-independent network of neurons embedded in the gut wall, which senses stretch from the food bolus and triggers the next segment of muscle to contract in sequence.

A concrete way to see this is to picture squeezing toothpaste from a tube: pressing the tube behind the toothpaste and releasing pressure ahead of it moves the paste toward the opening. Peristalsis achieves the same effect biologically and repeatedly, one segment at a time, so that swallowed food reaches the stomach even if you were lying down or upside down — gravity is not required.

Applying this concept helps explain several real digestive phenomena. Consider why swallowing a large, poorly chewed piece of food can cause pain or blockage: peristaltic waves have a limited diameter of constriction they can generate, so an oversized bolus may not be pushed through efficiently, causing the wave to stall or reverse (a reflex called antiperistalsis, associated with vomiting). This also explains why esophageal motility disorders, such as achalasia, cause swallowing difficulty — in achalasia, the coordinated relaxation ahead of the bolus fails, so the wave cannot move food into the stomach even though muscle contraction still occurs. Clinically, doctors diagnose such disorders using manometry, which measures pressure changes along the esophagus to confirm whether peristaltic waves are properly sequenced and whether the muscle relaxes appropriately at each point. Understanding peristalsis as a sequenced, two-muscle coordination problem — not just a single squeezing action — is the key to correctly diagnosing and treating conditions where the digestive tract fails to move food normally.
