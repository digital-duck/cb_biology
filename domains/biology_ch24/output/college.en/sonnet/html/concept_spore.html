Spore

A spore is a haploid reproductive cell that fungi (and some plants, algae, and bacteria) produce to disperse and propagate. In fungi, spores are typically formed through mitosis of an already-haploid parent, so a single spore, once released, can germinate and grow directly into a new multicellular haploid individual — no fertilization required. This is different from the sexual stage of a fungal life cycle, where two haploid cells fuse to form a diploid cell that later divides by meiosis to produce spores again. Both pathways end in spores, but the mitotic route simply copies the parent's genome, while the meiotic route shuffles genes and creates variation.

Worked example. Consider a mold like Rhizopus growing on bread. The visible black dots are clusters where thousands of spores are packed together and ready for release. Each spore carries a haploid genome identical to the parent that produced it, since mitosis preserves genetic content (barring mutation). When a spore lands on a new piece of bread with enough moisture, it germinates: the single cell divides repeatedly, growing into a network of fine threads that spreads across the surface — a fully multicellular, haploid fungal individual. No partner organism or fusion event was needed at any step.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a mycologist counts that one cluster releases 50,000 spores, and under controlled lab conditions 2% of spores successfully germinate into new colonies. How many new individuals arise from one cluster?

Set up the calculation as a straightforward proportion:

$$ \text{new colonies} = 50{,}000 \times 0.02 = 1{,}000 $$

This kind of reasoning matters in real contexts: agricultural scientists use germination-rate estimates like this to predict how fast a fungal pathogen (e.g., a crop rust) will spread across a field, while brewers and food scientists use the same logic in reverse — calculating how few surviving spores of a contaminant mold can still seed spoilage in a batch. In both cases, the key conceptual move is the same: because each spore is independently capable of producing an entire new haploid organism via mitosis, spore count directly scales with the number of potential new individuals, making spore output a practical proxy for a fungus's reproductive and dispersal capacity.
