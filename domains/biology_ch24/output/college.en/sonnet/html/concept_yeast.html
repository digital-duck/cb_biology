Yeast

Yeast is a unicellular fungus, meaning that unlike molds or mushrooms it typically lives its entire life as a single free-floating cell rather than as a branching, multicellular body. Most yeast species reproduce asexually through a process called budding: a small outgrowth forms on the surface of the parent cell, enlarges, and eventually pinches off as an independent daughter cell, leaving a scar on the parent's cell wall. Some species, most notably Candida albicans, are dimorphic — they can switch between a round, single-celled yeast form and an elongated, filamentous, multicellular form called hyphae, depending on environmental cues like temperature, pH, or nutrient availability. This switching ability is a form of phenotypic plasticity, allowing the organism to adapt its body plan without changing its genome.

Consider Saccharomyces cerevisiae, brewer's and baker's yeast, growing in a sugar-rich dough. Each cell absorbs glucose and metabolizes it, and under low-oxygen conditions it ferments the sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide — the gas that makes bread rise. As each cell approaches a critical size, it buds off a genetically identical daughter. Under favorable conditions this budding cycle can repeat roughly every 90 minutes, so a single founding cell can generate a colony of billions within days. This rapid, predictable division rate is precisely why S. cerevisiae is the standard model organism for studying the eukaryotic cell cycle: its budding events are visually countable under a microscope, making generation time easy to measure directly.

This growth behavior also has practical, quantitative payoff. Suppose a yeast culture doubles every 90 minutes and you start with 500 cells. To find the population after 6 hours, note that 6 hours contains four 90-minute intervals, so the population is $500 \times 2^4 = 8000$ cells — the same exponential-growth reasoning used for bacteria, radioactive decay, or compound interest. A brewer or biotechnologist uses this relationship in reverse: given a target cell density needed to start fermentation, they can calculate how many doubling cycles — and thus how much lag time — are required from a smaller starter culture, which is exactly how pitching rates are planned in industrial-scale beer and biofuel production.
