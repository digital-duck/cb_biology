Fungal Toxins

Fungi produce a remarkable diversity of toxic secondary metabolites, and humans are poisoned by them through two distinct routes. Mycotoxicosis occurs when toxins produced by molds contaminate stored food or feed — the fungus itself may be invisible or already dead, but its toxin persists. Mycetismus occurs when a person directly eats a poisonous mushroom (a macroscopic fungal fruiting body) containing toxins. The distinction matters practically: mycotoxicosis is a food-safety and agricultural-storage problem, while mycetismus is a foraging and identification problem.

Consider aflatoxin, produced by Aspergillus flavus growing on improperly stored grain, peanuts, or corn. Aflatoxin is a potent liver carcinogen, and it does not break down with normal cooking. This is why food-safety agencies set strict numerical limits — the U.S. FDA action level for aflatoxin in food is 20 parts per billion — and why grain is tested and rejected rather than "cooked to be safe." Contrast this with amatoxins, found in mushrooms of the genus Amanita (including the aptly named death cap). Amatoxins inhibit RNA polymerase II, shutting down protein synthesis in liver and kidney cells; symptoms are famously delayed 6–24 hours after ingestion, by which point irreversible organ damage may already be underway. The delay is itself dangerous: victims often feel fine initially, dismiss early gastrointestinal symptoms, and seek treatment too late.

For problem-solving, treat toxin exposure as a dose-and-pathway analysis rather than a simple "poisonous or not" label. Ask three questions: (1) Source — is this a storage-related mold toxin or a mushroom species toxin? This determines whether the fix is better storage/drying practices or better field identification. (2) Mechanism and target organ — aflatoxin targets the liver via DNA damage over long-term exposure; amatoxins target the liver acutely via enzyme inhibition. Knowing the target organ tells you what clinical markers to monitor (e.g., liver enzymes) and how urgently. (3) Threshold behavior — mycotoxin regulation relies on measurable concentration thresholds (ppb) because chronic low-dose exposure is the risk; mushroom poisoning has no safe threshold because a single meal can deliver a lethal dose. Applying this framework to a hypothetical scenario — say, a shipment of moldy corn versus a foraged mushroom of uncertain species — should lead you to different, appropriate actions: laboratory toxin testing in the first case, and expert taxonomic identification (or simple avoidance) in the second.
