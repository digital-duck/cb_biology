Fungus

A fungus is a eukaryotic, heterotrophic organism whose cell walls are built from chitin — the same structural polysaccharide found in insect exoskeletons — rather than the cellulose that gives plant cell walls their rigidity. This chemical distinction matters practically: it is why antifungal drugs can target fungal cell walls without harming human cells (which lack cell walls entirely) and why fungicides used in agriculture must be chemically distinct from herbicides. Unlike plants, fungi cannot photosynthesize, and unlike animals, they don't ingest food internally. Instead, fungi feed by absorptive heterotrophy: they secrete digestive enzymes onto or into their surroundings, breaking down complex organic matter externally, then absorb the resulting simple molecules through their cell membranes. Most fungi grow as networks of thread-like filaments called hyphae, whose collective mass is a mycelium — a structure that maximizes surface area for absorption.

Worked example: Consider a fungus like Rhizopus (bread mold) colonizing a slice of bread. It cannot swallow the starch molecules in the bread; they are too large and remain outside its cells. Instead, the fungus secretes amylase enzymes onto the bread surface. Amylase hydrolyzes starch into glucose, a small enough molecule to cross the fungal cell membrane via transport proteins. The fungus has effectively moved digestion outside its body — a strategy that lets it exploit a solid, immobile food source without needing to engulf it.

Problem-solving application: This absorptive strategy explains fungi's essential ecological role as decomposers. Suppose an ecologist finds that a forest floor accumulates less dead leaf litter in wetter years, despite similar litter input. A student asked to explain this should reason from the fungal lifestyle: enzyme secretion and nutrient absorption both require moisture, so higher humidity accelerates fungal decomposition rates, recycling carbon and nutrients faster into the soil. This same logic underlies practical decisions in food storage (why dry, sealed containers resist mold), agriculture (why fungal pathogens spread rapidly in humid greenhouses), and medicine (why fungal infections thrive in moist skin folds). Recognizing fungi's external-digestion mechanism, rather than memorizing them as "decomposers" by label, lets you predict and troubleshoot fungal behavior across very different real-world contexts.
