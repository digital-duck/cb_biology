Heterotrophic Nutrition

Fungi cannot photosynthesize — they lack chlorophyll and any internal mechanism for fixing carbon dioxide into sugar. Instead, they practice heterotrophic nutrition: acquiring both carbon skeletons and chemical energy by breaking down organic molecules that other organisms have already synthesized. What makes fungal heterotrophy distinctive is where digestion happens. Animals digest internally, taking food into a gut cavity before breaking it down. Fungi do the reverse: they secrete digestive enzymes (exoenzymes) into their surroundings, breaking complex polymers into small molecules outside the cell, then absorb those simple molecules directly across the plasma membrane. This strategy is called absorptive heterotrophy.

Consider a fungus growing on a fallen log. The wood is composed largely of cellulose and lignin — polymers far too large and insoluble to cross a cell membrane. The fungal hyphae (thread-like filaments) release cellulase and lignin-degrading enzymes into the wood. These exoenzymes break the polymers into glucose and smaller phenolic fragments right there in the substrate. The hyphae then absorb these products, using the glucose for both an energy source (via cellular respiration) and a carbon source for building new fungal biomass — chitin, proteins, and nucleic acids.

This mode of nutrition explains several practical patterns you can predict and test. First, because exoenzymes must reach into the substrate and products must return to the cell, fungi maximize their surface area by growing as branching networks of thin hyphae (a mycelium) rather than as a single compact body — the same surface-area-to-volume logic that governs why small organisms exchange gases efficiently. Second, absorptive nutrition constrains habitat: fungi thrive embedded within or directly against their food source (soil, wood, a host organism) because enzymes and absorbed nutrients cannot travel far. Third, this trait explains fungal ecological roles. Saprotrophic fungi (decomposers) break down dead organic matter, recycling carbon and nitrogen into ecosystems; parasitic fungi secrete exoenzymes into living host tissue; mutualistic fungi, as in mycorrhizae, exchange absorbed soil nutrients for plant-made sugars.

If you're evaluating whether an organism uses heterotrophic absorptive nutrition, ask two questions: does it lack chlorophyll/chloroplasts, and does it release digestive enzymes into its environment before nutrient uptake? A "yes" to both distinguishes fungi from both autotrophic plants and animals, which digest internally after ingestion.
